Tiny Habits

by BJ Fogg

The Small Changes That Change Everything

1

Motivation Is Unreliable

Why motivation fluctuates and why that makes it a terrible foundation

The Core Truth

Motivation is a wave. It comes and goes unpredictably. You feel fired up on Monday but deflated by Wednesday. This isn't a character flaw—it's how human motivation works. Building habits on motivation is like building a house on sand.

The secret to lasting change isn't willpower or motivation—it's making behaviors so small that motivation becomes irrelevant.
— BJ Fogg
The Problem

The motivation wave: You start a new habit with high motivation, but by week two, that motivation drops. Most people interpret this as personal failure. They think, "I'm not disciplined enough." In reality, they're experiencing a predictable pattern. Relying on motivation to sustain habits sets you up for failure.

Connection: Drive by Daniel Pink

Pink distinguishes between extrinsic motivation (external rewards) and intrinsic motivation (internal satisfaction). Fogg flips this: he says don't rely on motivation at all. Instead, design your behavior and environment so small wins feel automatic.

The Reality Check

Your New Year's resolution gym membership? The motivation wave crashed around January 15th. That's not you failing—that's neuroscience.

The Fogg Alternative

Instead of chasing motivation, focus on tiny behaviors you can do consistently and celebration that wires them into your brain. Remove motivation from the equation entirely.

2

The Fogg Behavior Model

B=MAP: Understanding the three ingredients that must converge

The equation that unlocks behavior change:

B = MAP
Behavior occurs when Motivation, Ability, and Prompt converge at the same moment.
Motivation (M)

Your desire to do the behavior. It's the wave we discussed—unreliable on its own. Fogg ranks it as least important of the three factors.

Ability (A)

Your capacity to do the behavior. Can you actually do this? Do you have the time, skills, energy, and resources? This is where Fogg focuses: make behaviors so tiny that ability becomes automatic.

Prompt (P)

A trigger that reminds you to do the behavior. It can be external (a notification) or internal (a feeling), but without a prompt, you forget. The prompt must be specific and timely.

When you design a behavior, all three elements—motivation, ability, and a prompt—must be present in the same moment for the behavior to occur.
— BJ Fogg, Tiny Habits
Key Insight

Fogg's model differs from Atomic Habits' cue-routine-reward loop. Fogg treats motivation as optional and emphasizes immediate celebration (the "shine") as the wiring mechanism for the neural pathway, not future rewards.

Make It Tiny

The two-pushups prescription: start smaller than feels worth it

The Strategy

Fogg's most radical insight: tiny isn't a compromise. It's the strategy. When you commit to two pushups (not fifty), you remove all psychological resistance. You can do it even on your worst day. This is not settling—this is smart design.

1
Ridiculously tiny: 2 pushups, 1 stretch, floss 2 teeth
2
Easy: 10 pushups, 3 stretches, floss all teeth
3
Feels like work: 25 pushups, full stretching routine
4
Too big: 100 pushups, 30-min gym session
Start with a version of the behavior that requires minimal motivation and ability. You can always scale up later. Right now, make it so easy you can't fail.
— BJ Fogg
Why This Works

Our brains are maladaptive. A goal that feels too ambitious triggers avoidance and shame. A goal you can crush? That builds momentum and confidence. Two pushups done every day beats fifty pushups attempted twice then abandoned forever.

The Truth About Willpower

You don't need willpower to do two pushups. You need willpower to do 50 and fail. Tiny behaviors sidestep the willpower problem entirely.

4

Anchor Habits

The recipe for automaticity: After I [anchor], I will [tiny behavior]

After I [existing behavior], I will [new tiny behavior].

This is the core structure. Your existing habit is the trigger. Your new behavior is linked to it like a chain.

How It Works

An anchor is an existing behavior you do every day without thinking: pouring your morning coffee, sitting down at your desk, brushing your teeth. By attaching a tiny new behavior immediately after the anchor, you leverage neurological momentum. The anchor becomes a trigger you don't have to remember.

Examples in Action
  • ✓ After I pour my morning coffee, I will do 2 pushups.
  • ✓ After I sit at my desk, I will write 3 sentences of my novel.
  • ✓ After I brush my teeth, I will floss 2 teeth.
  • ✓ After I close my laptop, I will stretch for 30 seconds.
The anchor is the key. When you find a behavior someone already does every day, you can attach new behaviors to it. The existing pathway in the brain becomes the highway for the new habit.
— BJ Fogg
Comparison: Atomic Habits (James Clear)

Clear calls this a "cue." Fogg calls it an "anchor." Same concept, different framing. Fogg's emphasis on finding behaviors you do automatically without thinking—rather than setting external reminders—makes the habit stick neurologically faster.

5

The Shine

Immediate celebration: the neurological mechanism for wiring habits

✨

After you do the tiny behavior, celebrate immediately.

The Science

Celebration triggers a release of dopamine in your brain, which wires the neural pathway connecting the anchor → behavior → good feeling. This is not psychology—it's neurology. "Good job" to yourself isn't silly; it's scientifically necessary for habit formation.

What Celebration Looks Like
  • • A fist pump
  • • "Nice!" said out loud
  • • A smile and deep breath
  • • A brief moment of pride
  • • A small reward (chocolate square)
Celebration is not frivolous. It's the mechanism your brain uses to rewire itself. When you feel good immediately after a behavior, your brain marks that behavior as worth repeating.
— BJ Fogg
This Is Not Self-Delusion

You might think celebrating two pushups is silly. But your brain doesn't care about the scale of the achievement—it cares about the feeling. Dopamine doesn't judge.

Connection: Dopamine Nation (Anna Lembke)

Lembke writes about dopamine dysregulation in a "hedonic treadmill" world. Fogg flips this: by celebrating small wins intentionally, you're harnessing dopamine for behavior change instead of chasing it externally. You're the one in control of the signal.

6

Designing Your Environment

The difference between motivation and design: make desired easy, undesired hard

The Core Principle

You can't motivation-yourself into consistency, but you can design your way there. Environment shapes behavior more reliably than willpower ever will. Reduce friction for desired behaviors. Increase friction for undesired ones.

Make Desired Behaviors Easier
  • ✓ Leave your workout clothes on the bed
  • ✓ Put your book on your pillow
  • ✓ Prep healthy snacks, not junk
  • ✓ Set phone reminders as anchors
  • ✓ Make the desired behavior the default
Make Undesired Behaviors Harder
  • ✗ Delete social media apps from your phone
  • ✗ Keep cookies off your counter
  • ✗ Leave your credit card in another room
  • ✗ Put your phone in a drawer
  • ✗ Add friction, not motivation
Environment design beats motivation every time. Stop asking yourself to be stronger. Instead, make your desired behavior the path of least resistance.
— BJ Fogg
Design vs. Discipline

Fogg's radical reframe: discipline isn't the answer. Good design is. If you keep failing at a habit, don't blame yourself—blame your environment. Then redesign it.

Growing Habits

From tiny to transcendent: the multiplication effect and when to scale up

The Natural Growth Pattern

You don't keep doing 2 pushups forever. Once the habit becomes automatic and celebrated, something magical happens: you naturally want to do more. This isn't goal-setting—it's organic expansion. The tiny behavior becomes the seed of a bigger one.

The Progression

Week 1: 2 pushups after morning coffee → celebrated. Week 3: 5 pushups → celebrated. Week 8: 20 pushups → celebrated. By month 3, you're doing a full strength routine, not because you forced it, but because the tiny foundation made it inevitable.

Don't scale up too fast. You'll break the celebration loop and motivation will return as a problem. Grow gradually and let the success feeling guide the pace.
— BJ Fogg
The Multiplication Effect

One tiny habit sticks → you feel confident → you anchor a second tiny habit → you feel more confident → you redesign your environment → suddenly you have a system. Small changes compound into a fundamentally different life, not through willpower, but through cascading design choices.

The Plot Twist

You start by saying "I'll just do 2 pushups." Six months later, you're the person doing pushups. Identity didn't come first—it came last.

Troubleshooting

When habits don't stick: the diagnostic toolkit

The Framework

When a tiny habit fails, don't assume you're broken. Instead, ask: Is the issue motivation, ability, or prompt? Fogg provides a diagnostic for each.

Problem: Low Motivation

Signal: You understand the habit but don't feel like doing it.

Solution: Make the behavior even tinier. If 2 pushups don't spark motivation, try 1 pushup. Or switch anchors. Or redesign the celebration.

Problem: Low Ability

Signal: You want to do it but can't (too hard, too tired).

Solution: Remove barriers. Do fewer reps. Do it earlier in the day. Simplify the behavior until it's effortless.

Problem: Missing Prompt

Signal: You keep forgetting. You're motivated and able, but the trigger never happens.

Solution: Find a better anchor (something you do more consistently). Set a phone reminder. Write it on a sticky note. Make the prompt impossible to miss.

Failure is information, not character. When a habit doesn't stick, you have data. Use it to adjust the system, not to punish yourself.
— BJ Fogg
Compassion First

Fogg emphasizes self-compassion in troubleshooting. Shame doesn't fix habits. Understanding does. When something doesn't work, be curious, not critical.

9

Untangling Bad Habits

Breaking the chains: why "just stop" doesn't work

The Problem with "Just Stop"

Willpower-based approaches tell you to quit cold turkey. But a bad habit is still a behavior governed by B=MAP. Your brain formed it because it met a need (motivation, ability, prompt all converged). Simply removing the behavior creates a void. You'll revert unless you address the underlying structure.

Fogg's Three-Pronged Approach
  1. Remove motivation: Don't demonize the habit. Understand why you do it. What need does it meet? Address that need differently.
  2. Reduce ability: Make the bad behavior harder. Remove triggers. Increase friction. Delete the apps. Change your environment.
  3. Eliminate prompt: Break the anchor chain. If you scroll social media after work, change when you finish work. If you snack while watching TV, don't keep snacks there.
You can't willpower your way out of a bad habit. But you can design your way out. Change the environment, remove the anchor, reduce the ability. The behavior will fade naturally.
— BJ Fogg
The Replacement Strategy

Don't just remove bad habits—replace them. Use the same anchor but attach a tiny good behavior instead. "After I finish work, instead of scrolling, I will walk to the window and take 3 deep breaths." Same trigger, better behavior.

The Unsolvable Math of Willpower

Your brain has mastered the bad habit. Fighting it requires constant energy (willpower). Designing around it requires zero energy once set up. Design wins.

Skills of Change

Habit design as a meta-skill you improve at

The Big Reframe

Habit change isn't about motivation or willpower. It's a skill you get better at with practice. The first habit is awkward. The third one is easier. By the tenth, you're fluent in the language of tiny habits, anchors, and celebration.

The Three-Step Process You Master
  1. Design tiny: You learn to shrink behaviors to their essence. What's the smallest, easiest version of what you want?
  2. Find an anchor: You become sharp at spotting existing routines in your day and locking new behaviors to them.
  3. Celebrate: You learn which celebrations actually trigger dopamine for you. A fist pump? A phrase? A moment of quiet pride?
The more habits you design, the better you become at designing them. This is why BJ trained 40,000 people—the goal wasn't just their habits, it was to teach them the skill of change itself.
— BJ Fogg
Fogg's 40,000-Person Experiment

Fogg ran a five-year study where he coached 40,000 people through designing tiny habits. The finding: those who mastered the skill of habit design continued changing habits long after the program ended. They didn't need Fogg anymore—they had internalized the process.

Mastery Compounds

The first habit takes conscious effort. By habit ten, it's automatic. By habit 50, you're designing complex behavioral systems instinctively. You've built the meta-skill of change.

The Role of Identity

How tiny habits reshape self-perception—and why it works differently than Atomic Habits

Aspect Atomic Habits (Clear) Tiny Habits (Fogg)
Identity Start with identity ("I'm a runner") Identity emerges from behavior
Sequence Identity → Behavior → Outcome Behavior (tiny) → Celebration → Identity shift
Motivation anchor Internal identity External anchor + immediate celebration
Scaling 1% better identity Organic growth from momentum
Fogg's Identity Theory

You don't become a runner by deciding you're a runner. You become a runner by running (tiny), celebrating, anchoring, and letting the behavior generate the identity. Say two pushups after coffee for 30 days, celebrate each one, and you'll start thinking "I'm someone who exercises." Identity isn't the prerequisite—it's the result.

Identity follows behavior. When you consistently do something small that you celebrate, your brain recodes who you are. You're not trying to become someone else—you're discovering who you already are becoming through your actions.
— BJ Fogg
Why This Matters

Many people try to adopt an identity before they have the habits to support it. Fogg reverses the order: create the micro-behaviors first, wire them through celebration, and identity shifts as a natural consequence. This removes the pressure of "pretending" and replaces it with genuine experience.

The Fogg-Clear Divergence

James Clear says "decide who you want to become" first. BJ Fogg says "start behaving and let identity catch up." Both work, but they start from opposite ends of the chain.

Ripple Effects

How one tiny behavior cascades into a transformed life

The Cascade

Start with 2 pushups. That becomes a "I exercise" identity. That influences your sleep (you're tired from exercise, sleep better). That affects your mood (better sleep, better mood). That makes you more social (better mood, reach out to friends). That creates accountability (friends ask about your progress, you stay consistent). One tiny behavior becomes a system.

The Golden Behaviors

Some tiny habits unlock disproportionate change. Fogg calls these "golden behaviors." For example: writing 3 sentences of a novel daily. This reshapes how you see yourself (writer), your daily structure (protected time), your confidence (measurable progress), your productivity (compound word count). One behavior, multiple ripples.

You're not just changing one habit. You're redesigning your habit garden. One seed grows. It shades the ground differently. Other seeds germinate. Soon you have an ecosystem.
— BJ Fogg
Designing Your Habit Garden

Think of your life as a garden. Don't try to plant everything at once. Choose your first seed (tiny habit) based on potential ripples. What one behavior, if wired through celebration and anchored consistently, would cascade into other positive changes? Start there. Let the system grow organically.

The Domino Effect Nobody Expects

You're not trying to change your life. You're trying to do 2 pushups. But your brain doesn't know the difference between a small change and a big one—it just knows it's getting better at something. And that feeling spreads to everything else.

Fogg's Core Promise

This is why tiny habits work: they seem small because they are. But they're small in scope, not in impact. The neurological and psychological ripples are profound. You're not trying to transform your life—you're planting seeds. The transformation is inevitable.

Stories from the Field

The Flossing Revolution

BJ Fogg's own journey began with a simple question: "How do I floss more?" He was already flossing occasionally, but inconsistently. Instead of relying on motivation, he designed: After I brush my teeth (anchor), I will floss 2 teeth (tiny behavior). Then he celebrated. Just "Good!" to himself. Within a week, he forgot to celebrate—so he did it anyway because the trigger was automatic. Within a month, he was flossing all his teeth without conscious effort. The tiny habit expanded organically because the foundation was solid. The point: his dental health transformed not through willpower, but through systems design.

The 40,000-Person Validation

Fogg ran a five-year experiment with 40,000 participants, tracking which tiny habits stuck and which didn't. The patterns were clear: those who found good anchors and celebrated immediately had an 80% success rate at month three. Those who tried to build habits on motivation alone had less than 20% success. Those who started too large gave up within two weeks. The data proved what Fogg hypothesized: tiny, anchored, celebrated behaviors are the reliable path to lasting change. And remarkably, those 40,000 people didn't just change one habit—they learned the skill of habit design and continued designing new ones long after the study ended.

The Morning Transformation

One of Fogg's participants, Sarah, struggled with mornings. She'd hit snooze five times, rush through breakfast, skip exercise, and arrive at work frazzled. She tried everything: alarm clocks across the room, pre-planned workouts, motivational podcasts. Nothing stuck. Then she designed: After I drink my first cup of coffee (anchor), I will do 2 pushups (tiny). She celebrated with a fist pump each time. Two weeks in, it was automatic. Month two, she was doing 10 pushups and felt more energized. That energy rippled: she started saying good morning to her kids instead of rushing. She ate breakfast instead of skipping it. Her sleep improved because she was less stressed. One tiny habit—2 pushups—cascaded into a completely different morning. The lesson: design one behavior brilliantly, and the system reorganizes around it.

Your Tiny Habits Playbook

Step 1: Choose Your First Tiny Habit

Pick one behavior you want to do. Make it absurdly small. Not "exercise daily." Not "get fit." Two pushups. One stretch. Floss two teeth. Write one sentence. The smaller the better.

Step 2: Find Your Anchor

Identify an existing behavior you do every day without thinking. After I brush my teeth. After I pour my morning coffee. After I sit at my desk. After I close my laptop. The anchor must be consistent and automatic.

Step 3: Link Them Together

After I [ANCHOR], I will [TINY BEHAVIOR]. Write it down. Say it out loud. Make it specific and exact. Not "exercise after coffee." "After I pour my coffee, I will do 2 pushups."

Step 4: Practice & Celebrate

Do it. Tomorrow. Then the next day. After each time, celebrate immediately. A fist pump. "Nice!" said aloud. A smile. 30 seconds of pride. This wires the neural pathway. Do this for 30 days.

Step 5: Troubleshoot If Needed

If it doesn't stick, ask: Is it a motivation problem (try celebration)? An ability problem (make it tinier)? A prompt problem (find a better anchor)? Adjust and try again.

Step 6: Let It Grow

Once the tiny habit is automatic (usually 2-3 weeks), it will naturally expand. You won't need to force it—your brain will want more. Let it grow organically. Then design your next tiny habit.

Start so small that the behavior is trivial. Then rely on celebration, anchoring, and environmental design to make it stick. The magic isn't in the size of the behavior—it's in the consistency and the celebration that wires it in.
— BJ Fogg

The Core Promise

You don't need more willpower. You don't need to be more disciplined. You don't need to motivation-hack your way through life. What you need is to design behaviors so small that they're automatic, anchor them to existing routines, celebrate immediately, and let the system do the work. That's the secret to lasting change.
— BJ Fogg
Why This Works

For decades, self-help told you to rely on motivation and willpower. But neuroscience shows that's backwards. Your brain changes through repeated behaviors + emotional reward (celebration). Tiny habits create the perfect conditions for both. They're easy enough to do even on your worst day. They're small enough that celebration feels natural. They're anchored so you don't have to remember them. And when they stick, they cascade into bigger changes you never planned.

The Path Forward

You don't have to be perfect. You don't have to transform your life overnight. You don't have to believe in yourself more or want it harder. You just have to choose one tiny behavior, anchor it, celebrate it, and let the system work. That's enough. And it always has been.

A Radical Thought

What if the reason you've struggled with habits isn't because you're broken? What if it's because you've been asked to rely on the wrong thing—motivation—instead of the right things: design, celebration, and anchoring? Fogg's answer: you were never the problem. The system was.
