Lesson 1

The MAP Theory of Humor

Material, Audience, Performance: The Foundational Triad

Key Lesson

What is MAP?

Comedy is not a solitary act. It exists at the intersection of three critical elements: Material (what you say), Audience (who's listening), and Performance (how you deliver it). Shatz & Helitzer argue that understanding this triad is fundamental to comedy writing.

Material

Your content—the jokes, observations, and stories. Material can be original observations, topical humor, personal anecdotes, or clever wordplay. Great comedy material often comes from surprising juxtapositions, exaggeration, or revealing hidden truths.

Audience

The specific group receiving your humor. Different audiences find different things funny. A joke that kills with college students might bomb with retirees. Understanding your audience's demographics, shared references, and values is crucial. Are they urban or rural? Young or old? What's their education level? What do they care about?

Performance

How you deliver the material matters as much as what you say. Timing, pacing, vocal inflection, facial expressions, physical movement—all of these elements can make or break a joke. The same joke told differently by different people will get different laughs.

Funny Take: MAP in Action

The Same Joke, Different MAP Outcomes

Material: "I told my therapist I had suicidal thoughts. He said, 'That'll be $200.' I said, 'I only have $50.' He said, 'Come back when you can afford it.'"

Scenario A (Good MAP): Told by a comedian with timing and experience to an adult audience that appreciates dark humor. Gets big laughs because the audience appreciates the absurdist logic and the tension-release.

Scenario B (Poor MAP): Same joke told casually to a room of people who don't know you and aren't expecting comedy. Performance lacks the setup and timing to make it land. Audience doesn't trust the comedic intent. Falls flat or offends.

The Difference? Not the material itself, but the match between material, audience expectations, and performance delivery.
AI Connection

Why Computational Humor Struggles with MAP

Current AI humor generation models can produce joke structures (setup + punchline), but they struggle enormously with MAP because they lack social understanding. An AI can write "Why did the chicken cross the road?" but it can't intuitively understand which audience would laugh hardest, or how to modulate performance-equivalent properties like timing and emphasis in text.

Large language models like GPT have trained on comedy, so they can mimic joke patterns statistically. But true MAP theory requires real-time audience reading and dynamic adjustment—the ability to sense when a joke isn't landing and pivot delivery accordingly. This requires social cognition that current AI systems lack. Some researchers are exploring how audience response data (laughter tracking, demographic information) could be fed into models to improve comedy generation, but this remains an open research problem.

Furthermore, humor is deeply tied to shared cultural context. An AI trained on predominantly English-language comedy may not understand the specific references, taboos, and values of a niche audience. This is why computational humor research increasingly focuses on context-aware generation rather than generic joke production.

Cross-Book Connection

Born Standing Up by Steve Martin

Steve Martin's memoir repeatedly illustrates MAP theory in action. He discusses testing material with different audiences, adjusting his delivery based on crowd size and mood, and how his "absurdist" material only worked when he found the right audience. His performance evolution shows how all three MAP elements must evolve together.

"Comedy is not about having a great joke—it's about understanding your audience so deeply that they laugh before you even speak."

Lesson 2

The THREES Formula

Setup, Anticipation, Punchline: The Trinity of Comic Timing

Key Lesson

Breaking Down the THREES Structure

The THREES formula is perhaps the most fundamental building block of comedy. It consists of three distinct components that work together to create laughter:

  1. Setup: Establishes a premise, rule, or expectation. The audience's brain creates a pattern. Example: "I went to the doctor yesterday."
  2. Anticipation: The middle ground where the audience waits for the punchline. This is where tension builds. "He said I had six months to live."
  3. Punchline: The unexpected break from the established pattern. "I said, 'I can't pay the bill.' He said, 'Okay, you have a year.'"

The power of THREES lies in pattern recognition and expectation-violation. The human brain automatically completes patterns, so when a comedian sets up a pattern and breaks it unexpectedly, the surprise creates cognitive dissonance that manifests as laughter.

Funny Take: THREES in Real Comedy

Classic THREES Examples Broken Down

Setup: "My wife told me to take out the trash."
Anticipation: "So I did."
Punchline: "She said, 'That's not what I meant.'"

Why it works: The audience expects "take out the trash" literally (expected action), but the punchline reframes the setup to show marital miscommunication.
Setup: "I'm reading a book on the history of glue."
Anticipation: "Can't put it down."
Punchline: [Silence—the anticipation IS the punchline]

Why it works: Audience anticipates a traditional joke, but the punchline is a pun embedded in the statement itself.
Setup: "I told my girlfriend she was drawing her eyebrows too high."
Anticipation: "She looked surprised."
Punchline: [No punchline—the setup IS the joke]

Why it works: The audience's brain completes the pattern (girlfriend upset = expected), but the pun "looked surprised" violates this by being literal rather than emotional.
AI Connection

The THREES Formula and Computational Humor

The THREES formula is one area where AI has made genuine progress in humor generation. Because it's a structural pattern, AI can be trained to recognize and generate THREES-compliant jokes through pattern matching and substitution. Many joke generation algorithms work by: (1) learning common setup patterns, (2) identifying expectation fields, and (3) generating unexpected-but-coherent punchlines.

Researchers at MIT, Stanford, and other institutions have developed models that can generate thousands of puns and setup-punchline jokes by understanding semantic relationships and incongruities. The model learns that setups establish semantic fields (like "doctor" → "medical context"), and punchlines work by introducing unexpected elements from different semantic domains. However, this still produces mediocre humor because it doesn't account for delivery, cultural relevance, or deeper incongruity beyond simple word relationships.

Cross-Book Connection

Thinking, Fast and Slow by Daniel Kahneman

Kahneman's research on System 1 (fast, intuitive thinking) and System 2 (slow, deliberate thinking) explains why THREES works. The setup engages System 1, which creates automatic pattern completion. The punchline surprises System 1, causing a momentary error correction that triggers laughter. This is why unexpected punchlines work better than obvious ones—they exploit the gap between what System 1 predicted and what actually happened.

"A joke is a tiny story with a lie in the middle."

Lesson 3

Hostility as the Humor Engine

The Dark Truth: Aggression, Superiority, and Laughter

Key Lesson

The Aggressive Core of Comedy

This lesson explores a controversial but essential truth: much of comedy contains an element of hostility. This isn't to say all comedy is mean-spirited, but rather that a significant portion of humor derives from aggression, mockery, or triumph over someone or something.

Forms of Comedic Hostility

  • Direct Mockery: Making fun of a specific person, group, or characteristic. "Why do programmers prefer dark mode? Because light attracts bugs."
  • Self-Deprecation: Turning hostility inward. "I'm so bad at dating, my love life is a Netflix tragedy series."
  • Superiority Jokes: Establishing yourself as superior. Jokes about stupid people, incompetent politicians, or fumbling celebrities.
  • Schadenfreude: Laughing at others' misfortune. Pratfalls, embarrassing moments, failures of the powerful.
  • Subversion: Attacking norms, authorities, or sacred cows through mockery.

Shatz & Helitzer argue that recognizing hostility in humor isn't about judgment but about understanding its mechanism. A comedian needs to know when they're using hostility, toward whom, and to what effect.

Funny Take: Hostility Examples

Aggression in Comedy: Masked and Unmasked

Overt Hostility: "My ex-wife still lives in the past. I have to pay child support for a kid she's raising with my lawyer. I'm not even sure which one she's sleeping with."

This joke works by mocking multiple targets: the ex-wife, the lawyer, and the situation's absurdity. The hostility is obvious and intentional.
Masked Hostility: "I love my wife. I really do. But if she spent as much time cleaning the house as she spends criticizing how I clean it, we could see our kitchen floor."

This appears gentle but contains subtle criticism: the wife is both picky and lazy. The hostility is sugar-coated with "I love my wife," which makes the aggression more acceptable to audiences.
Self-Directed Hostility: "I'm terrible with money. I tried to save for retirement, but I spent it on therapy to deal with having no retirement savings."

Here, the comedian is the target of hostility, making fun of themselves. This creates laughter through self-awareness and vulnerability.
AI Connection

Teaching AI About Comedic Hostility

One of the hardest problems in computational humor is teaching AI when hostility is appropriate. AI systems trained on comedy datasets learn to produce aggressive jokes at high rates because hostility is statistically common in stand-up, roasts, and satirical content. But this creates ethical problems: models may generate jokes targeting protected groups, or jokes that are hostile in ways that violate social norms.

This is why modern AI humor systems require careful fine-tuning and constraint layers. Researchers are developing "harm detection" systems that can identify when a generated joke crosses ethical lines. Some approaches use human feedback to teach models which types of hostility are acceptable (punching up at power) versus unacceptable (punching down at vulnerable groups). Others train models on categorized datasets that separate mean-spirited jokes from clever ones.

The deeper research question is: Can we teach AI the difference between hostility that creates cathartic laughter (roasting a powerful person) versus hostility that perpetuates harm (stereotyping a marginalized group)? This requires embedding cultural and ethical knowledge into the model, which is an unsolved problem in AI ethics.

Cross-Book Connection

The Comedy Bible by Judy Carter

Carter explores how comedians can use hostility ethically—punching up rather than down, targeting systems rather than people, and using self-deprecation to build audience connection before launching into more aggressive material.

"Every laugh contains an element of superiority—the triumph of the teller over the topic."

Lesson 4

Incongruity Theory: The Clash of Expectations

Laughter as Cognitive Dissonance Resolution

Key Lesson

What is Incongruity?

Incongruity theory posits that humor arises when two incompatible ideas, concepts, or contexts collide unexpectedly. The greater the gap between what we expect and what actually happens, the funnier the joke (up to a point).

The Incongruity Framework

  • Expected Pattern: Audience anticipates a logical, predictable outcome based on the setup.
  • Unexpected Reality: The punchline introduces something from a completely different context or logic system.
  • Cognitive Collision: The audience's brain experiences a moment of confusion as it tries to reconcile the two incongruent ideas.
  • Resolution: The audience finds a way to integrate both elements, and the relief of this cognitive resolution triggers laughter.

Incongruity differs from hostility theory because it focuses purely on the structural relationship between ideas, not on superiority or aggression.

Funny Take: Incongruity in Action

Examples of Incongruity-Based Humor

Setup (Establishes Pattern): "I gave up alcohol, sugar, and fried food."
Expectation: "I must feel amazing, healthier, more energetic."
Punchline (Incongruity): "So I've got a lot of time to think about how much I miss alcohol, sugar, and fried food."

Incongruity: The expected benefit (feeling better) collides with the unexpected reality (psychological suffering). The gap between health goals and emotional reality is the humor.
Setup: "I told my son about the birds and the bees."
Expectation: "A serious, awkward talk about reproduction and relationships."
Punchline: "He said, 'Dad, I meant the Hornets and the Lakers.'"

Incongruity: One context (sex education) collides with another (sports teams). The mismatch of what "birds and bees" means in different contexts creates the laugh.
Setup: "I'm on a seafood diet."
Expectation: "A healthy eating regimen focused on ocean foods."
Punchline: "I see food, and I eat it."

Incongruity: "Seafood" (a diet type) collides with "see food" (a homophone with different meaning). The semantic shift creates unexpected logic.
AI Connection

Computational Models of Incongruity

Incongruity is easier for AI to model than hostility because it's more structural. Researchers have developed "incongruity detection" systems that work by mapping semantic spaces. The idea is that words and concepts exist in high-dimensional semantic space (derived from language models like Word2Vec or transformer embeddings). A setup establishes a path through this space, and a good punchline deviates sharply from that path.

These models can measure incongruity by calculating semantic distance: How far apart are the words/concepts in the setup versus the punchline? Larger distances correlate with stronger incongruity. Some research groups have built humor generation systems that maximize incongruity while maintaining semantic coherence—finding punchlines that are unexpected but still "make sense" retroactively.

However, AI struggles with what researchers call "incongruity resolution"—understanding how audiences mentally bridge the gap between the incompatible concepts. Humans can hold multiple meanings simultaneously and enjoy the cognitive tension. AI systems often fail to generate incongruities that resolve satisfyingly, resulting in jokes that feel random or nonsensical rather than funny.

Cross-Book Connection

Thinking, Fast and Slow by Daniel Kahneman

Kahneman's exploration of cognitive biases and pattern recognition directly explains incongruity. The brain is a pattern-matching machine. When incongruity is introduced, it violates the pattern, creating cognitive load. The resolution of this load (finding a way to understand both pieces) produces a pleasurable "aha!" moment that can manifest as laughter.

"Humor is the collision of two truths that have no business meeting."

Lesson 5

Superiority Theory: The Triumph Principle

Why We Laugh at Others: The Psychological Engine of Comedy

Key Lesson

The Power Dynamic of Laughter

Superiority theory, dating back to Aristotle and refined by philosophers like Hobbes and Schopenhauer, posits that we laugh to assert dominance or superiority. When we laugh at a joke, we're momentarily experiencing ourselves as superior—smarter, more competent, or more morally righteous than the subject of the joke.

How Superiority Works in Comedy

  • Target Identification: The comedian (or the audience) identifies a target—could be a person, group, stereotype, or institution.
  • Inferiority Establishment: The setup establishes the target as foolish, incompetent, hypocritical, or wrong in some way.
  • Triumph: The audience laughs, experiencing a momentary sense of superiority over the target.
  • Social Bonding: Everyone laughing together creates in-group status—we are smarter/better than those being mocked.

Shatz & Helitzer teach that understanding superiority theory helps comedians write material that works because it resonates with a fundamental human drive: the desire to feel good about ourselves relative to others.

Funny Take: Superiority in Comedy

Classic Superiority-Based Jokes

"Did you hear about the new restaurant on the moon? Great food, no atmosphere."

Superiority Target: The listener (momentarily). They fall for the setup expecting a literal answer, then you're superior for delivering the pun.
"I'm not saying my wife is stupid, but she thought the Bermuda Triangle was a new hip-hop group."

Superiority Target: The wife. The teller establishes themselves as more knowledgeable/intelligent by mocking her ignorance.
"Politicians are the only people who can lie, get caught, apologize, and then lie about the apology."

Superiority Target: Politicians. The audience feels morally superior for seeing through political hypocrisy.
AI Connection

Teaching AI Superiority Dynamics

AI systems can be trained to recognize and generate superiority-based humor by learning target identification patterns. Machine learning models can infer who or what is being mocked, and what inferiority is being attributed. However, this creates serious ethical challenges.

If trained on comedians who punch down at marginalized groups, AI learns to perpetuate harmful stereotypes. This is why researchers are increasingly focused on "inclusive AI humor" that generates superiority-based jokes that punch up (at powerful targets) rather than down. Some approaches use reinforcement learning with human feedback to reward jokes that target institutions/behaviors rather than identity groups.

The fundamental challenge: Teaching AI to distinguish between cathartic superiority (laughing at a politician's hypocrisy) and harmful superiority (laughing at someone's immutable characteristics) requires encoding complex social values into the model.

Cross-Book Connection

The Comedy Bible by Judy Carter & Born Standing Up by Steve Martin

Both Carter and Martin discuss the ethics of superiority—when it's acceptable to make fun of people and when it crosses the line. The best comedians use superiority strategically, creating jokes where the audience feels good about themselves for laughing, not guilty afterward.

"Every joke is a tiny victory for the person laughing."

Lesson 6

The Rule of Three: Comedy's Magic Number

Pattern, Pattern, Rupture: The Most Powerful Comedy Structure

Key Lesson

Why Three is Sacred in Comedy

The Rule of Three is perhaps the most versatile and reliable comedic structure. Present three items, ideas, or actions—the first two establish a pattern, and the third breaks it unexpectedly.

The Mechanics of the Rule of Three

  • First Item: Establishes a category or pattern. "My therapist said I was avoiding my problems..."
  • Second Item: Reinforces the pattern, making it feel predictable. "...so I changed therapists."
  • Third Item: Breaks the pattern in an unexpected way. "Then I changed countries."

Two items create a pattern but don't feel complete. Four items become tedious. Three items hit the sweet spot where the brain recognizes a pattern and anticipates continuation—then violates that expectation.

Funny Take: The Rule of Three Examples

Perfect Implementation of Triadic Comedy

"I have three hobbies: drinking coffee, overthinking, and misplacing my keys."

Pattern: Two relatable activities established. Third item: physical object (unexpected category shift). Creates humor through incongruity.
"I'm reading a book on the history of glue. I'm on chapter one, I can't put it down. Chapter two, still can't put it down. Chapter three—I'm stuck to the book."

Pattern: Pun-based setup (can't put it down = good book). Third iteration takes it literally (physically stuck), escalating the joke.
"My ex-wife took the house, the car, and the dog. I said, 'This is the worst day of my life.' She said, 'No, wait—I'm taking our son too.' I said, 'Actually, you know what? Take me. Please.'"

Pattern: Items being taken (house, car, dog, son). Third item: The speaker himself, escalating the absurdity and self-deprecation.
AI Connection

The Rule of Three in Machine Learning Humor

The Rule of Three is one of the most predictable comedy structures, which makes it ideal for AI training. Machine learning models can learn pattern-establishment and pattern-breaking by analyzing datasets of jokes. Many computational humor systems explicitly encode the Rule of Three as a structural template: [Category 1], [Category 2], [Unexpected Violation].

Researchers have built systems that generate Rule-of-Three jokes by: (1) selecting a semantic category, (2) generating three members of that category, (3) making two semantically coherent, and (4) making the third incongruent but contextually relevant. The results are mediocre jokes, but statistically valid structures.

The limitation is that truly great Rule-of-Three jokes require escalation, thematic coherence, and cultural knowledge that goes beyond structural templates. An AI might generate "I like pizza, I like pasta, and I like traffic violations," which follows the structure but has no logical connection between the first two items and the third.

Cross-Book Connection

Made to Stick by Chip & Dan Heath

The Heaths discuss the power of lists in memorable communication—three items are easier to remember than four or five. This principle extends to comedy: three-part structures are more memorable and satisfying than longer sequences.

"One is a fact. Two is a coincidence. Three is a joke."

Lesson 7

POW: Play On Words

Puns, Double Meanings, and Semantic Hijacking

Key Lesson

The Art of Linguistic Comedy

POW encompasses puns, homophones, homonyms, double entendres, and any joke that derives humor from the ambiguity, multiple meanings, or sound similarities of language itself.

Types of POW

  • Homophones: Words that sound identical but have different meanings. "I told my wife she was drawing her eyebrows too high. She looked surprised."
  • Double Meanings: A word that has multiple legitimate definitions. "I'd tell you a chemistry joke, but I know I wouldn't get a reaction."
  • Portmanteaus: Blending two words together. "I'm reading a book on philosophy called 'Immanuel.' It's by Kant."
  • Phonetic Ambiguity: Words that sound like phrases. "I stayed in a piano bar. I asked the bartender for a drink. He said, 'Sure, what's your name?' I said, 'Ben.' He said, 'Nice to meet you, Ben. We have some C-sharp and B-flat over here.'"

POW is popular because it exploits the natural ambiguity of language. The brain can hold multiple meanings simultaneously, creating the cognitive tension that produces laughter.

Funny Take: POW in Comedy

Examples of Wordplay Mastery

"I used to hate facial hair, but then it grew on me."

POW Type: Double meaning. "Grew on me" = both "became more acceptable to me" AND "literally grew on my face."
"Time flies like an arrow. Fruit flies like a banana."

POW Type: Phonetic ambiguity. First sentence: "time" flies (moves fast) like an arrow. Second sentence: actual fruit flies like bananas. The parallel structure makes the shift hilarious.
"A man walks into a bar. He walks up to the bartender and says, 'Give me a drink.' The bartender says, 'You look like you need one.' The man says, 'Actually, I just walked into that door.' The bartender says, 'Yeah, I know. That happens a lot. We keep it here to keep people entertained.'"

POW Type: Phonetic ambiguity / homophone. "Bar" = drinking establishment AND wooden barrier. Joke subverts expectations.
AI Connection

Computational Wordplay and NLP

POW is one of the most amenable areas of comedy to AI because it's fundamentally a language puzzle. Modern NLP systems can identify homophones, homonyms, and multiple word meanings using semantic embeddings. Researchers at several universities have built pun generators that work by identifying words with multiple meanings and creating setups that establish one meaning while the punchline exploits another.

SemEval 2017 and 2021 included competitions for computational humor where pun generation was a major task. Winning systems used techniques like: (1) identifying homonyms in a dataset, (2) building setups that activate the first meaning, (3) generating punchlines that activate the second meaning while maintaining semantic coherence.

However, AI-generated puns are often low-quality because they maximize wordplay at the expense of thematic coherence. The pun might be technically correct but nonsensical contextually. Great human-written puns work because the multiple meanings make thematic sense, creating what some researchers call "coherent incongruity."

Cross-Book Connection

The Comedy Bible by Judy Carter

Carter discusses the relationship between wordplay and personal voice—some comedians build careers on puns and wordplay (like Henny Youngman), while others treat it as a supporting technique. The key is knowing when POW serves the joke versus when it becomes the joke.

"The best pun is one where you didn't see it coming, but you can't unsee it once it's there."

Lesson 8

Double Entendre & Layered Comedy

Making Jokes That Work on Multiple Levels

Key Lesson

The Architecture of Multi-Level Humor

A double entendre is a phrase that has two interpretations, usually one innocent and one suggestive (often sexual). More broadly, layered comedy is any joke that works on multiple levels—yielding different laughs depending on the audience's knowledge, sensitivity, and frame of mind.

Layers of Comedy

  • Surface Level: The obvious interpretation that any audience can understand. "That's what she said."
  • Second Level: A deeper meaning that requires more contextual knowledge. Understanding pop culture references, historical context, or insider information.
  • Third Level: Meta-humor—jokes about the nature of jokes themselves, or commentary on comedy structure.
  • Fourth Level: Personal/autobiographical context that changes meaning based on the comedian's known history.

Great comedy often works on multiple levels simultaneously, allowing different audience members to laugh for different reasons.

Funny Take: Layered Humor Examples

Jokes That Work on Multiple Levels

"I'm reading a book about anti-gravity. It's impossible to put down."

Level 1 (Surface): Pun on "put down" = can't stop reading.
Level 2 (Literal): Anti-gravity means objects stay floating.
Level 3 (Meta): The joke is about the joke—it's a simple pun that everyone gets.
"My wife said, 'Why are you always so sarcastic?' I said, 'Oh, I'm sorry. I didn't realize that question had a sincere answer.'"

Level 1 (Surface): Sarcastic response to a question about sarcasm.
Level 2 (Relational): Anyone in a sarcasm-heavy relationship recognizes this dynamic.
Level 3 (Logical): The joke reveals that the wife's question is designed to trap the speaker—any sincere answer would contradict their nature.
"I'm not superstitious, but I am a little stitious."

Level 1 (Surface): Funny wordplay on "stitious" as a partial word.
Level 2 (Linguistic): Understanding that "superstitious" contains "stitious" as a morpheme.
Level 3 (Meta): The joke is about the structure of language and how we parse words.
AI Connection

Multilevel Humor and AI Comprehension

This is where AI struggles most in humor. Current language models can identify surface-level jokes—simple setups and punchlines. But they lack the cultural context, social intelligence, and meta-cognitive awareness needed to generate jokes that work on multiple levels.

To generate a joke that works on three levels, an AI would need to: (1) understand the surface meaning, (2) identify an alternative meaning, (3) find a third interpretation, and (4) ensure all three are activated simultaneously by the same text. This requires a level of semantic sophistication and intentional design that current models can't achieve. Most AI-generated jokes collapse to single-level wordplay rather than elegant multilayered structures.

Some researchers are exploring how to fine-tune language models on carefully annotated datasets of multilayered jokes, teaching the model to generate multiple coherent interpretations. But this remains an unsolved problem in computational humor research.

Cross-Book Connection

Impro by Keith Johnstone

Johnstone discusses how improvisational comedy creates layers through audience knowledge and callbacks. A callback—repeating a joke from earlier with a new context—creates multiple layers because the original meaning combines with the new situation.

"The best jokes work for the person who's never heard your material and the person who's heard all your material."

Lesson 9

Callbacks and Tag Lines

Extending Laughter Through Repetition and Amplification

Key Lesson

The Power of Returning to Established Jokes

A callback is a reference to a previously established joke or premise, recontextualized in a new situation. A tag (or top line) is an additional punchline added after the initial punchline. Both extend comedy through delayed gratification and pattern reinforcement.

How Callbacks Work

  • Initial Joke: "My wife left me because I was obsessed with my career."
  • Callback (Later in Set): "The sad thing is, my therapist said, 'You need to focus on your relationships.' So I did. Now she's my ex-wife."

How Tags Work

  • Initial Joke: "I went to the doctor. He said, 'You have six months to live.' I said, 'I can't pay my bill.' He said, 'Okay, you have a year.'"
  • Tag: "He said, 'Come back next week with your wife.' I said, 'Why?' He said, 'I want to tell her the good news.'"

Callbacks create in-group bonding—only people who remember the initial joke get the callback. Tags maximize the energy of a successful joke by adding another punchline before the audience stops laughing.

Funny Take: Callbacks and Tags in Action

Extended Joke Structures

Initial Setup: "I tried online dating. My profile said I was looking for someone who could make me laugh."
Punchline: "I got a message from a woman who said, 'I can make you laugh.' I said, 'How?' She said, 'Have you seen your profile picture?'"
Tag: "So I changed my picture. Now nobody messages me at all. I guess I WAS the only funny thing in my profile."

The tag extends the joke by revealing a new angle—if he removes his ugly picture, he loses all appeal, suggesting his ugliness was actually his charm. Layered and self-deprecating.
First Joke (20 minutes into set): "I love my wife, but she's terrible at giving directions. We went to the restaurant, and she said, 'Turn right at the Olive Garden.' I said, 'There are five Olive Gardens on this street!' She said, 'Yeah, but this is the one we're going to.'"
Callback (40 minutes later): "We went to couples therapy yesterday. The therapist asked my wife, 'What do you think is your biggest weakness?' She said, 'I think I could be better at communicating.' I said, 'No, it's your sense of direction.' The therapist asked HOW she got my attention initially. She said, 'I told him to turn right at the Olive Garden.' The therapist said, 'And he did?' I said, 'I'm still looking for it.'"

The callback recontextualizes the original joke in a new setting (therapy), adds a new character (the therapist), and escalates the absurdity. People who remember the initial joke laugh harder because they catch the reference.
AI Connection

Computational Callbacks and Long-Form Structure

Callbacks require maintaining context over long time spans—remembering jokes from 20 minutes earlier and finding ways to reference them. This is difficult for traditional machine learning models because it requires encoding long-range dependencies in memory.

However, transformer-based language models (like GPT) are better suited to this task because they can attend to distant tokens in long sequences. Researchers have explored using fine-tuned GPT models to generate callbacks by: (1) extracting key elements from previous jokes, (2) identifying new contexts where these elements apply, and (3) generating new punchlines that reference the original setup while introducing novel elements.

The challenge is that callbacks require narrative coherence and audience memory—they only work if the original joke is sufficiently memorable and the callback is sufficiently original to feel surprising. Most AI-generated callbacks either too directly repeat the original joke or deviate so far that the connection is lost.

Cross-Book Connection

Impro by Keith Johnstone

Johnstone discusses how improvisers use repetition to build momentum. Repeating a joke in a new context (like callbacks) trains the audience to expect variations, making new additions more surprising and rewarding.

"A callback turns the audience from spectators into accomplices—they're part of the in-joke."

Lesson 10

The Take: Comedy's Unit of Measurement

Timing, Pause, and the Performance Art of Delivery

Key Lesson

What is a Take?

A "take" is the smallest unit of comedic delivery—it's the performer's reaction to something unexpected or absurd. It involves a pause, a look, a shift in facial expression or body language that says "Did you notice what just happened?" The audience laughs not just at the joke but at the performer's recognition of the joke's absurdity.

Anatomy of a Take

  • The Setup: Something absurd or unexpected is stated matter-of-factly.
  • The Pause: A beat of silence where the audience processes the absurdity.
  • The Reaction: The performer reacts—a double-take, raised eyebrow, deadpan stare, or verbal acknowledgment.
  • The Payoff: The audience laughs at the performer's reaction, not just the joke itself.

The take is particularly important in sketch comedy and character-based comedy. It's what separates a joke delivered mechanically from a joke delivered with comic awareness.

Funny Take: Timing and Takes

Examples of Takes in Performance

Setup: "So I bought a used car from my barber."
[Long pause + blank stare]
Audience thinks: "Wait, why would you buy a car from a barber?"
Performer (continuing matter-of-factly): "The brakes didn't work, so he gave me a refund."
[Another pause, slight head tilt as if realizing the absurdity]
Audience laughs: Not just at the non-sequitur (barber/car), but at the performer's recognition that this is ridiculous.
Setup: "I was at the bank, and the teller asked, 'Do you want large bills?'"
[Long pause, raised eyebrow]
Performer (deadpan): "I said, 'No, I prefer my bills to be humble and modest.'"
[Beat, direct eye contact with audience]
The take here: The performer is acknowledging that they understood the bank teller's question literally (asking about the physical size of bills) but answered it as if it was about the moral character of the money. The pause and the eye contact say, "I know this is stupid, and you know this is stupid, and we both know I know you know."
AI Connection

Computational Performance and Timing

This is one of the hardest aspects of humor to model computationally because timing is inherently performative. While an AI can generate text (the joke), it can't perform the take. If an AI were generating a script for a comedian, it could annotate where pauses should occur, but it would still require human performance to work.

Some researchers are exploring how to generate annotated comedy scripts where timing information is embedded. Others are working on text-to-speech systems that can modulate delivery in comedically appropriate ways—adding pauses, changing intonation, and adjusting pacing. But this remains a frontier area in computational humor.

The deeper issue is that takes require awareness of audience expectations and real-time adjustment. A comedian reads the crowd and adjusts the pause length based on how quickly they caught the joke. This real-time feedback loop is something current AI systems can't replicate.

Martin talks about how he spent years developing his comic timing, learning to understand audience expectations so he could violate them at precisely the right moment. Johnstone discusses how improv timing creates surprise—by delaying or accelerating reactions, improvisers keep audiences off-balance.

Lesson 11

Format-Specific Comedy Writing

Stand-up, Sketch, Sitcom, Roast: Adapting Comedy to Its Container

Key Lesson

Different Formats, Different Rules

Comedy doesn't exist in a vacuum. The format—whether stand-up, sketch, sitcom, or roast—fundamentally changes how you write, structure, and deliver jokes. Shatz & Helitzer teach that great comedy writers understand how to adapt their material to the format's specific constraints and opportunities.

Stand-Up Comedy

Constraints: One person, live audience, real-time reaction, minute-by-minute pacing. Opportunities: Direct connection with audience, ability to adjust material based on crowd response, intimate confession possible. Key Techniques: Callbacks work well; audience memory is active. Tags extend successful jokes. The take is essential. Building a persona matters because the audience is evaluating you as a person.

Sketch Comedy

Constraints: Multiple characters, need for visual comedy, limited time (usually 3-7 minutes), structured narrative. Opportunities: Can build absurd worlds; costumes/props enhance comedy; multiple characters create dynamic interactions. Key Techniques: Premise-driven humor (establish the ridiculous situation, then milk it). Object work and physical comedy matter. Sketches need clear beginnings and ends. The best sketches have an internal logic—the absurdity is internally consistent.

Sitcom/TV Comedy

Constraints: Written format, must work in 22 or 42-minute episodes, ensemble cast, commercial breaks, repeating characters/locations. Opportunities: Can build ongoing relationships between characters; visual gags; production values (camera angles, editing); callbacks across seasons. Key Techniques: Character consistency matters because viewers know these people. Jokes are woven into plot, not just standalone. Setup/punchline structures are tighter because pacing is controlled by editing. Humor often comes from character behavior rather than explicit jokes.

Roasts

Constraints: Target is present, jokes are personal/harsh, audience expects aggression, short time limit. Opportunities: Hostility is not just acceptable, it's expected and encouraged. Callbacks to the target's known characteristics are effective. Physical presence and confidence matter. Key Techniques: Research the target thoroughly so jokes are specific, not generic. Balance personal attacks with self-deprecation. The best roasters punch up and down—they mock the target but also themselves for caring enough to roast them.

Funny Take: Format-Specific Comedy

The Same Premise in Different Formats

Premise: A man is bad at relationships

Stand-Up Version: "I'm terrible at relationships. My therapist asked me, 'What's your biggest fear?' I said, 'That I'll die alone.' She said, 'I have good news and bad news. Good news: you definitely won't die alone. Bad news: it'll probably be because someone killed me in self-defense after dating you.'"

Format-Specific Elements: Direct address to audience, self-deprecation, callback to the therapist, the take (pause after "bad news" for effect), audience complicity.
Sketch Version: [Two characters: Man at a dating coaching service. Coach is ridiculously optimistic despite the man's hopelessness.]
Coach: "Okay, so you're going to smile, make eye contact, and ask her about herself."
Man: [Tries to smile; it's a grimace] "Like this?"
Coach: "No. Teeth. You should show teeth."
Man: [Shows all his teeth like a skeleton] "Better?"
Coach: "Significantly worse. Actually, let's skip the smile. How about we just work on not telling people about your ex-wife immediately?"
Man: "But she's a great reference!"

Format-Specific Elements: Visual comedy (bad smile), multiple characters creating dynamic, absurd internal logic, repetition and escalation, prop-based humor (coaching tips that gradually lower expectations).
Sitcom Version: [Recurring character Jake repeatedly makes dating mistakes across the season. Early episode: he goes on a date but spends the whole time talking about his ex. By mid-season, he's learned nothing. In the finale, he's set up on a date and is silent for five minutes because he's trying so hard not to mention his ex. His date says, "So... do you have nothing else to talk about?" He says, "I'm afraid if I open my mouth, the first word that comes out will be her name."]

Format-Specific Elements: Character growth (or lack thereof), callbacks to earlier episodes, character consistency, humor from situation rather than explicit jokes, ensemble dynamic with other characters reacting to Jake's behavior.
AI Connection

Training AI on Format-Specific Humor

One of the promising directions in computational humor is format-specific generation. Rather than building a single generic joke generator, researchers are training separate models for different formats: stand-up, sketch, puns, etc. The idea is that different formats have different structural requirements, and models can learn these patterns from training data specific to each format.

For example, sketch comedy models are trained on scripts from SNL, MADtv, and other sketch shows. These models learn that sketches have particular narrative structures: introduction of absurd premise, escalation, callbacks to earlier beats, and a resolution (or sometimes no resolution, just an absurd ending). Stand-up models learn the setup-punchline-callback structure and the role of audience interaction.

However, this approach still requires massive amounts of high-quality training data and sophisticated model architecture. And it still doesn't capture the performance element—an AI can generate a sketch script, but the sketch only works if performed well by competent actors.

Others are character actors who excel in sitcoms but struggle with personal stand-up material. Understanding format requirements helps writers and performers match their strengths to the right vehicle.

Lesson 12

Editing Jokes: The Craft of Cutting

Trimming, Tightening, and Distilling Comedy to Its Essence

Key Lesson

The Art of Comedic Minimalism

A good joke is efficient. Every word serves a purpose. Shatz & Helitzer teach that editing is where amateurs become professionals. It's not about adding more jokes—it's about removing everything that doesn't contribute to the laugh.

Principles of Joke Editing

  • Remove Explanations: If the audience "gets it," you don't need to explain it. Each explanation word delays the next joke.
  • Cut Unnecessary Setup: Establish the minimum context needed for the punchline to work. "I went to a restaurant. It was bad. The waiter was rude. The food was cold." Just say: "The waiter brought my food and said, 'Be careful, the plate is hot.' I said, 'But the food's cold.' He said, 'Yeah, that's why I warned you about the plate.'"
  • Trim Modifiers: "My old, tired, overworked therapist said..." becomes "My therapist said..." The modifiers don't add to the joke.
  • Eliminate Hedging: "I think, maybe, kind of...failed at online dating" becomes "I failed at online dating." Weak words undermine the joke.
  • Tighten the Rhythm: Joke pacing is as important as phrasing. Short setups, punchy punchlines. Long, meandering setups kill the timing.
Funny Take: Before and After Editing

How Editing Improves Jokes

BEFORE (Unedited):
"So I was at the grocery store last week, and I was buying some groceries. I had a lot of items, and the line was really long. There were a lot of people in front of me. And so the cashier, she was this older woman, and she looked kind of tired, you know? And she said to me, 'Paper or plastic?' And I said, 'I don't know, what do you think is better for the environment?' She said, 'Honestly, I don't really care anymore.'"

AFTER (Edited):
"I asked the grocery cashier, 'Paper or plastic—which is better for the environment?' She said, 'Honestly, I stopped caring about the environment in 1987.'"

Changes: Removed unnecessary setup details (location details, line length, cashier appearance). Cut the vague "you know?" Tightened the punchline by adding a specific date, which makes the resignation funnier. Removed explanatory phrases. Result: Same joke, 1/3 the length, 3x the laughs.
BEFORE:
"My wife and I went to marriage counseling because we were having some communication issues. You know, we weren't really talking to each other very much. And the therapist asked us, 'What do you think is the main problem in your marriage?' And I said something, and my wife said something different, and we both disagreed, obviously. And the therapist said, 'I see the problem now.' And she said, 'You two don't listen to each other at all.'"

AFTER:
"In couples therapy, the therapist asked, 'What's your main problem?' My wife and I answered simultaneously with completely different things. The therapist said, 'I see—you two have a fundamental communication issue.' My wife said, 'What?' I said, 'She wasn't listening.'"

Changes: Established the problem through action (answering simultaneously) rather than explanation. Added a tag that demonstrates the problem rather than stating it. Cut all filler and hedging language.
AI Connection

Computational Models for Joke Optimization

AI is surprisingly good at certain aspects of comedic editing. Language models can identify redundant words and suggest cuts. Some researchers have trained models to evaluate joke quality based on efficiency—measuring the "comedy density" of a joke (laughs per word). By this metric, trimming unnecessary words improves the joke.

However, AI struggles with what might be called "comedic minimalism"—the art of leaving just enough ambiguity that the audience enjoys figuring out the joke. Sometimes a seemingly unnecessary word creates rhythm or sets up a pattern that makes the punchline work. Cutting mechanically can destroy the joke's effectiveness.

Current research focuses on human-in-the-loop editing: AI suggests cuts, a human evaluates whether they preserve the joke's essence. This combination is more effective than either alone because humans understand comedic intent while AI can identify structural redundancies.

The Heaths' discussion of simplicity and clarity applies directly to joke editing. Removing unnecessary complexity makes ideas more memorable and impactful. The same principle works for comedy: removing unnecessary words makes jokes more memorable and their punchlines more impactful.

"Kill your darlings. If a phrase doesn't make the joke funnier, it makes it slower."
Comedy Writing Principle

Story Spotlight: The "What If" Game

One of the most powerful comedy writing tools is the "What If" game. Shatz & Helitzer teach that the most original comedy comes from asking strange questions and following them to their logical conclusions.

For example: "What if birds had thumbs?" instantly opens comedic territory. You can explore: how would they hold things? Would they use smartphones? Would they evolve to operate cars? Would human-bird relations change? Each "what if" branch leads to specific, explorable scenarios.

Another example: "What if your therapist billed you by the minute, and you could see the timer ticking?" You immediately envision jokes: paying more for longer silences, trying to get your money's worth by talking faster, the therapist literally speeding up their speech to avoid expensive sessions.

The "What If" game is powerful because it removes the pressure to be immediately funny. Instead of forcing a punchline, you're just exploring a hypothetical world. Jokes emerge naturally from the exploration. This is why improv is so valuable to comedy writers—improv IS the "What If" game in real-time, and it trains writers to build jokes from scenarios rather than forcing comedy.

Great comedy writers spend time journaling "what ifs." What if mirrors could lie? What if gravity worked sideways? What if everyone had to wear their insecurities on their shirt? The best premises come from questions that sound absurd at first but reveal hidden truths about how the world actually works.

Story Spotlight: Comedy = Tragedy + Time

One of the most profound insights in comedy theory is the formula: Comedy = Tragedy + Time. It means that the worst things in life become hilarious once enough time has passed for the emotional pain to fade.

A fresh wound is tragic. But the same event, told with proper distance and processing, becomes comedy. A friend who fell down the stairs yesterday and broke their leg isn't funny. But that same friend, recovering and telling the story three months later, is hilarious—especially if they can demonstrate the absurdity of how the fall happened or their own foolishness in the lead-up.

This principle explains why comedians often draw from their worst moments. Steve Martin's early comedy heavily featured stories about his failures and embarrassment. He transformed trauma into art by processing it through the lens of time and irony. The audience doesn't laugh at his pain—they laugh at his recognition of how ridiculous the situation was.

For comedy writers, this means: (1) Your personal disasters are comedy gold, but only once you've processed them. (2) The best comedy often comes from slightly traumatic experiences that you've had enough distance from to examine clearly. (3) The emotional truth of a painful moment can be the foundation of a powerful joke, as long as the joke demonstrates that you've moved beyond the pain.

This is also why audiences connect with vulnerable comedy—when a comedian shares something that hurt them and then laughs at it themselves, the audience recognizes they're watching someone who's processed their trauma. It's powerful because it's real.

Comedy Writing Cheatsheet

MAP Theory

Material: What you say. Audience: Who's listening. Performance: How you deliver. All three must align for comedy to work.

THREES Formula

Setup, Anticipation, Punchline. First two establish pattern, third breaks it. Fundamental building block of joke structure.

Hostility Theory

Much comedy contains aggression—mockery, superiority, schadenfreude. Recognize when you're using hostility and toward whom.

Incongruity Theory

Humor arises from collision of incompatible ideas. Greater gap = bigger laugh (to a point). Audience bridges the gap = relief = laughter.

Superiority Theory

We laugh to assert dominance. Joke identifies target as inferior, audience feels superior. Creates in-group bonding.

Rule of Three

Two items = pattern. Three items = complete structure. Pattern-pattern-rupture is the most reliable comedy structure.

POW (Wordplay)

Exploit language's ambiguity: homophones, double meanings, portmanteaus. Works because brain holds multiple meanings simultaneously.

Double Entendre

Phrases with two interpretations. Layered comedy works on multiple levels—surface, contextual, meta, personal.

Callbacks & Tags

Callback: Reference previous joke in new context. Tag: Additional punchline after initial punchline. Both extend laughter.

The Take

Smallest unit of comedic delivery. Pause + reaction = acknowledging the absurdity. Essential in sketch and character comedy.

Format Matters

Stand-up: Real-time adjustment, callbacks, persona matter. Sketch: Absurd premise + milk it. Sitcom: Character + plot. Roast: Hostility + research.

Editing Rule

Every word serves the laugh. Cut unnecessary setup, explanations, modifiers, hedging. Short setups, punchy punchlines. Efficiency = better timing.

"What If" Game

Ask absurd questions, follow to logical conclusions. Jokes emerge naturally from scenario exploration. Train your brain to build from premises.

Comedy = Tragedy + Time

Worst moments become hilarious with distance and processing. Draw from personal disasters once you've moved past them.

AI Humor Limits

AI struggles with: cultural context, real-time audience adjustment, performance timing, ethical hostility, incongruity resolution, multilayered humor. Excels at: structural templates, wordplay, pattern matching.

Cross-Disciplinary Learning

Kahneman (System 1 surprise), Johnstone (timing), Martin (presence), Carter (ethics), Heath (simplicity), all inform comedy writing.

The Core Truth

Comedy is fundamentally about surprise and truth. You establish expectations (through MAP, through THREES, through genre conventions), and then you violate those expectations in a way that reveals something true about the world. The audience laughs at the surprise, but they remember the truth. This is why the best comedians are often the most insightful about human nature—they're not just funny, they're *right* about something important.
