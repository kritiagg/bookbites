Everybody's Funny

Comedy is a learnable skill, not a gift from the comedy gods

🎭 KEY LESSON

Comedy is not a gift; it's a learnable muscle. Judy Carter's revolutionary insight: anyone can become funny through deliberate practice, observation, and discipline. The myth of "born funny" keeps millions from discovering their comedy voice.

  • Hard truth + Attitude = Joke: Take something painful or true about your life (hard truth), deliver it with a specific attitude (weird, scary, hard, stupid), and you have comedy. This formula works for EVERYONE.
  • Carter's Comedy DNA: She proved this by taking students from surgeons to accountants to corporate executives and turning them into working comedians within weeks. The Comedy Workout classes at UCLA transformed lives.
  • The Muscle Metaphor: Comedy works like any physical skill. You don't start as a world-class dancer; you train daily, develop technique, and build muscle memory. Same with joke writing and delivery.
😂 FUNNY TAKE

"They say 'You're either born funny or you're not' — the same people who say 'You're either born good at math or you're not,' then wonder why they tip their accountant in disappointed silences. Funny is just failure with better timing."

🤖 AI CONNECTION

LLMs are trained through supervised learning — they're not "born" with comedy ability either. They learn through exposure to data patterns, feedback, and fine-tuning. The process mirrors Carter's insight: comedy emerges from consistent training on examples, not magical genius. Both humans and AI benefit from explicit instruction on the formula (hard truth + attitude = funny) rather than hoping talent appears.

📚 CROSS-BOOK CONNECTION

Atomic Habits (James Clear): Carter's "comedy is learnable" mirrors Clear's thesis that tiny changes lead to remarkable results. Comedy is habit-forming, built through daily micro-practices. Mindset (Carol Dweck): The fixed mindset ("I'm not funny") vs. growth mindset ("I can learn to be funny") is Carter's core argument.

"Comedy is not something you're born with. It's something you learn, like tennis. You can take lessons."
— Judy Carter, The Comedy Bible

The Comedy Workout

Daily exercises for building your comedy muscle — morning pages, observation journals, and comedy reps

💪 KEY LESSON

Comedy requires a daily practice regimen. Carter prescribes three core exercises that form the Comedy Workout: Morning Pages (free-write to generate material), Observation Journals (capture life's friction), and Comedy Reps (deliver jokes repeatedly to build confidence).

  • Morning Pages (inspired by The Artist's Way): Write 3 pages of stream-of-consciousness every morning. No editing, no judgment. This unlocks your authentic voice and comedic perspective. Most comedians skip this; Carter says they're leaving gold on the table.
  • Observation Journal: Catalog the things that annoy you, confuse you, or feel absurd. That grumpy grocery clerk, airline seat width, dating app bios — these everyday truths become your joke fuel. Carter's students who kept observation journals had 10x more material after 30 days.
  • Comedy Reps: Write 5 jokes/day, no matter what. Some will be terrible; most will be. But you're building the neural pathways of joke construction. Comedians like Dave Chappelle and Jerry Seinfeld still do 5+ hours of material writing weekly, even after decades.
😂 FUNNY TAKE

"If you don't do Morning Pages, you're basically waiting for comedy inspiration to strike like lightning. That's called hoping. The workout people call it discipline. They also have HBO specials, so maybe try the pages."

🤖 AI CONNECTION

Large language models are trained on massive datasets of human-generated text. Carter's "Morning Pages" principle is exactly how LLM training works: exposure to vast amounts of unfiltered, diverse writing. The Observation Journal mirrors the data labeling process where patterns are extracted from raw experience. Comedy Reps are like inference-time optimization — you're testing and refining outputs repeatedly.

📚 CROSS-BOOK CONNECTION

Deep Work (Cal Newport): Carter's workout is a master class in deliberate practice. The Artist's Way (Julia Cameron): Morning Pages originate here, and Carter applies Cameron's creativity principles to comedy specifically.

"You must write every day. Comedy is a habit, not a hobby. Treat it like your job, even before it is."
— Judy Carter, The Comedy Bible

Hard Truth + Soft Joke

Take something painful, deliver it with comedy — the formula for confessional humor that lands

🎯 KEY LESSON

The best comedy comes from honest pain, wrapped in a soft joke delivery. Carter teaches that confessional humor works because the audience recognizes the truth underneath. You're not making light of trauma; you're making light WITH trauma as your collaborator.

  • Vulnerability = Relatability: When Hannah Gadsby talks about self-harm in her comedy, audiences don't laugh at the pain — they laugh at the honesty and the perfectly-timed punchline. The hard truth (pain) makes the joke land harder because it's REAL.
  • Ownership Model: Carter emphasizes: own your flaws, your failures, your weirdness. Louis C.K.'s jokes about being a bad parent work because he's genuinely critical of himself. The soft delivery says "I'm laughing at myself," which gives the audience permission to laugh with you, not at you.
  • The Contrast Effect: Dark truth + light joke delivery = cognitive surprise = laughter. Patton Oswalt talks about his dead mother with a smile and an absurd accent. The contrast creates the comedy.
😂 FUNNY TAKE

"If you're going to air your trauma on stage, you better make it FUNNY, or you're just a sad person yelling in a microphone. And there's only so much audience seating available for that."

🤖 AI CONNECTION

In adversarial training for language models, the "hard truth" is the raw, unfiltered prompt or problematic behavior. The "soft joke" is the refined output that addresses the concern with humor or creativity rather than brittleness. The model learns to handle difficult inputs gracefully — the same principle Carter teaches: don't ignore hard truths, transform them through the lens of comedy.

📚 CROSS-BOOK CONNECTION

Dare to Lead (Brené Brown): Both Brown and Carter argue that vulnerability is strength, not weakness. Storyworthy (Matthew Dicks): Similar emphasis on mining personal pain for universal storytelling truth.

"The truth is funny. Lies are just sad. Find your truth, own it, and deliver it with style."
— Judy Carter, The Comedy Bible

The Attitude Joke

Pick an attitude — weird, scary, hard, or stupid — and generate jokes from any topic

✨ KEY LESSON

The four attitudes are your comedy lenses. Every topic can be approached from 4 distinct angles: WEIRD (absurd, surreal), SCARY (threatening, dangerous), HARD (difficult, unfair), STUPID (foolish, ridiculous). Pick one, commit to it, and the jokes flow.

  • WEIRD: David Mitchell, Tig Notaro. The absurdist angle. "Dating apps are weird because you're essentially shopping for humans in a catalog. We've commodified romance in the most bizarre way."
  • SCARY: Hannah Gadsby, Maria Bamford. The threat angle. "Anxiety is scary because you're literally terrified of things that haven't happened yet. Your brain is writing horror fiction in real-time."
  • HARD: Hannah Einbinder, Damon Young. The struggle angle. "Adulthood is hard because nobody teaches you how to negotiate bills or find a therapist, but everyone expects you to know these things."
  • STUPID: Jim Gaffigan, Nate Bargatze. The self-deprecating angle. "I'm stupid because I spent $200 on a water bottle to drink the same water that comes from my tap."
😂 FUNNY TAKE

"If you can't decide which attitude to use, that means you don't have a perspective yet. And comedy without perspective is just observations, which is what your Uber driver does for the entire ride. Don't be the Uber driver."

🤖 AI CONNECTION

In prompt engineering, "system prompt" conditioning (the attitude) determines how an LLM responds to the same input. You can prompt an LLM to be weird, scary, harsh, or silly — just like Carter's four attitudes. The model's output changes dramatically based on the "attitude" set in the system prompt. Comedians are essentially prompt engineers: they set their attitude and generate variations on a topic.

📚 CROSS-BOOK CONNECTION

Show Your Work (Austin Kleon): The four attitudes are a taxonomy for creative perspective. Steal Like an Artist (Austin Kleon): Developing your attitude is how you develop your unique voice.

"Pick an attitude, any attitude. That decision alone separates a joke from a rant."
— Judy Carter, The Comedy Bible

The Act-Out

Become the character in your joke — physicality, voices, and facial expressions turn telling into showing

🎬 KEY LESSON

Show, don't tell. Physical comedy transforms a setup into an experience. The difference between describing your mom being angry and BECOMING your mom (voice, face, posture) is the difference between a chuckle and a laugh-out-loud moment.

  • Physicality: Russell Peters, Jim Carrey, Ali Wong. They don't just describe situations; they BECOME them. A high voice for his girlfriend, a deep voice for the angry dad, a slouch for the exhausted mom. Your body is part of the joke.
  • Vocal Shifts: Voices are comedy gold. A 6-foot-tall man squeaking like a toddler, or lowering his voice to a conspiratorial whisper, or adopting an accent — these tools bring characters to life on stage.
  • Facial Expressions: Silence can be funny. A perfectly-timed eye roll, a confused squint, a look of betrayal — Carter teaches that your face communicates as much as your voice. Some of the biggest laughs come without words.
😂 FUNNY TAKE

"If you're just standing there talking about something that happened, you're a podcast. Congratulations, you're NPR. But if you ACT it out, if you become the character, suddenly you're a comedian and the audience is WATCHING instead of half-listening while they check their emails."

🤖 AI CONNECTION

Multimodal AI (LLMs + voice synthesis + video generation) mirrors the act-out principle. Text-only responses are like "telling." But when you add tone of voice, facial expressions, body language, and video — you're "showing." The same prompt, delivered with different modalities, creates different emotional impact. Comedy relies on this: the same joke performed flatly vs. performed with physicality creates entirely different responses.

📚 CROSS-BOOK CONNECTION

The Moth (storytelling): "Show, don't tell" is a core principle of all storytelling. Body Language (Joe Navarro): Physical communication underlies human connection and comedy.

"Don't describe the character. BE the character. Your body is part of the punchline."
— Judy Carter, The Comedy Bible

Finding Your Voice

Authentic persona beats generic comedian — your specific POV is your superpower

🎭 KEY LESSON

Find the intersection of your personality, perspective, and pain. That's your voice. Don't try to be Seinfeld or Dave Chappelle. Be the person nobody else can be.

  • Specificity is Power: John Mulaney's voice is "confused, nostalgic 90s kid observing modern life." Hannah Gadsby's is "brutally honest, introspective, queer." Patton Oswalt's is "geek culture obsessive, loss-stricken dad." These aren't borrowed; they're deeply personal. That's why they're unmistakable.
  • Your Weirdness is Your Brand: The things about you that seem "too weird" for comedy are precisely what make you valuable. Nora Dunn talks about being Iranian-American and a recovering addict. Bo Burnham talks about depression and parasocial relationships. Their "weird" is universal because it's REAL.
  • Authenticity Over Impression: New comedians try on personas (the edgy guy, the clean guy, the self-deprecating guy) like costumes. Carter says: stop. Your authentic self is exponentially funnier than your impression of someone else's brand.
😂 FUNNY TAKE

"Trying to be a generic comedian is like trying to be a generic human. You already ARE one. Nobody needs another generic comedian. The world has plenty of those. But it has zero of YOU. So be weird. Be you. That's literally your only competitive advantage."

🤖 AI CONNECTION

Fine-tuning an LLM on specific data gives it a unique "voice" — a characteristic way of responding that's distinct from the base model. Your comedy voice is like fine-tuning your personality on your lived experience. The model without fine-tuning is generic; the fine-tuned model has character. Same principle: specific training data (your life) creates unique output (your comedy).

📚 CROSS-BOOK CONNECTION

Show Your Work (Austin Kleon): Finding your voice requires showing your work and being specific. Atomic Habits (James Clear): Your voice is built through small, consistent acts of authenticity.

"Nobody can be you better than you. Your perspective, your voice, your life — that's your comedy DNA. Stop trying to be someone else."
— Judy Carter, The Comedy Bible

Mix Tape to Set List

Structuring 5 minutes of comedy — opening, callbacks, transitions, and the out

🎵 KEY LESSON

Structure a tight 5-minute set like a song: strong open, main story, callbacks, and a final out. Carter teaches that rambling comedians lose audiences. Structure wins.

  • The Strong Open (0:00-0:30): Your first 30 seconds determine everything. A killer joke, a bold statement, or a surprising setup. You have 30 seconds to prove the audience should listen. Jerry Seinfeld's "These pretzels are making me thirsty" is a perfect open — immediate, relatable, funny.
  • The A-Story (0:30-3:00): Your main bit. A story about dating, your job, your family — told with the attitude joke formula. This is your meat. Dave Chappelle's bits often run 8-15 minutes because his A-stories are so compelling that audiences follow him anywhere.
  • Callbacks (3:00-4:30): Revisit a setup from earlier in the set with a new punchline. "Remember when I said X? Well, guess what..." This signals structure and rewards attentive listeners. It's how you build a "set" instead of a collection of jokes.
  • The Out (4:30-5:00): End on a huge laugh. Your best joke. Your strongest physical gag. Leave them wanting more. Never peter out. Comedians who end with their third-best joke are leaving money on the table.
😂 FUNNY TAKE

"If your set has no structure, you're just a person with trauma and a microphone. Structure is what turns trauma into a career. It's the difference between 'open mic guy' and 'comedian.'"

🤖 AI CONNECTION

Language models use attention mechanisms and positional encoding to track relationships between tokens across long sequences. A well-structured comedy set does the same: the open primes the audience for attention, the A-story maintains engagement, callbacks redirect attention backward (showing memory and structure), and the final out delivers maximum impact. Without structure, the "sequence" breaks down and the audience loses track.

📚 CROSS-BOOK CONNECTION

The Moth (storytelling): Story structure follows the same open-conflict-resolution arc. Made to Stick (Chip & Dan Heath): Callbacks and callbacks are "stickiness" — they make ideas memorable.

"A set without structure is just a person complaining. Structure is what turns complaining into comedy."
— Judy Carter, The Comedy Bible

Getting Stage Time

The open mic ecosystem — how to find mics, network, and embrace the grind years

🎤 KEY LESSON

The path to the stage is unglamorous but clear: open mics, clubs, networking, and years of grinding. There's no shortcut. But there IS a map. Carter lays it out ruthlessly.

  • Finding Mics: Open mics are free. They're held in bars, coffee shops, clubs. Sign up, get your 5 minutes. Do 2-3 mics per week minimum. Louis C.K. did 300+ mics a year for 15+ years. That's not exceptional; that's the baseline.
  • Barking: When other comics perform, "bark" — laugh loudly, encourage the audience, create energy. This builds community and helps the comic. Comics who bark get help when they're on stage. Comics who don't get silence.
  • Networking: The funny people you meet at mics are your future collaborators, producers, friends. Dave Chappelle's early career was built on relationships with other young comedians. Nate Bargatze has talked about comedians helping each other through the grind.
  • The Grind Years: 1-3 years of open mics. 3-5 years of club gigs for door money or drink minumums. Only THEN do agents notice. Only THEN do TV appearances happen. The overnight success is a myth. Jerry Seinfeld did 300+ club gigs before his first TV appearance.
😂 FUNNY TAKE

"If you're waiting for your big break to happen, you're going to wait a very long time. Meanwhile, the person doing mics every night is getting better, building material, and making friends. One of you will get a Netflix special. Spoiler: it's not the person waiting."

🤖 AI CONNECTION

Large language models improve through iterative training on diverse data (open mics), feedback loops from human evaluators (barking and audience response), and fine-tuning (working your tight material in clubs). There's no "instant expert" mode. Only repetition, feedback, and adaptation. The "grind years" for comedians mirror the training epochs for neural networks.

📚 CROSS-BOOK CONNECTION

Atomic Habits (James Clear): 1% daily improvements over years create mastery. Deep Work (Cal Newport): The grind years are deep work in its purest form.

"You don't become a comedian overnight. You become one after 500 open mics, 100 club gigs, and the willingness to bomb in front of 40 drunk people."
— Judy Carter (paraphrased), The Comedy Bible

Writing for TV/Film

Spec scripts, writer's rooms, sitcom structure — translating stage comedy to the screen

📺 KEY LESSON

TV comedy has a rigid structure: cold open, A-story, B-story, tag. Unlike stage comedy where you control the pacing, TV writers work within constraints. But those constraints force clarity.

  • Cold Open (0:00-2:00): A 2-minute scene that's totally disconnected from the plot, designed purely to get a laugh. Friends always opened with something at Central Perk. The Simpsons opens with the couch gag. This primes the audience to expect comedy.
  • A-Story: The main plot. Usually involves the lead character facing an obstacle or desire. The Simpsons: Homer gets fired/gets a new job. Friends: Ross does something awkward with Rachel.
  • B-Story: A subplot for secondary characters. This is where the absurdity lives. While the A-story is grounded (and mostly emotional), the B-story can be ridiculous because it doesn't need to be emotionally resolved.
  • Tag/Button: The final 30-60 seconds. A joke that sends the audience out laughing. Carter emphasizes: never underestimate the power of a strong tag. It's what they remember.
😂 FUNNY TAKE

"Sitcom writers wake up thinking about act breaks. Stage comedians wake up thinking about their life. One gets network TV money; the other gets coffee from a gas station. The difference is structural discipline and the ability to write jokes on a deadline while a showrunner yells at you in a room."

🤖 AI CONNECTION

Structured prompting in LLMs (giving step-by-step instructions, specifying output format, separating tasks into acts/scenes) improves output quality dramatically. A sitcom writer giving detailed act-by-act notes is essentially doing prompt engineering for actors and screenwriters. The AI Connection: complex problems (comedy writing) benefit from structure (prompts with clear acts, beats, character arcs). Unstructured requests produce meandering outputs, just like unstructured screenplays.

📚 CROSS-BOOK CONNECTION

Save the Cat (Blake Snyder): Story structure underlies all narrative. The Anatomy of Story (John Truby): Character needs drive plot, which drives comedy.

"In stand-up, you control the pacing. On TV, the structure controls you. Learn to love the structure. It will set you free."
— Judy Carter (paraphrased), The Comedy Bible

Selling Your Comedy

The business side — agents, showcases, festival submissions, and the entertainment industry machinery

💼 KEY LESSON

The comedy business is a funnel: headshot → demo reel → showcase → agent → TV/film → special. Each step requires specific materials and strategic positioning. Most comedians skip steps or do them half-heartedly. That's why most never get representation.

  • Headshot: Professional photo. Not a selfie. Not a photo from your cousin's wedding. A real headshot that makes you look likeable and professional. This goes on your website, agent submission, and festival applications.
  • Demo Reel (1-3 minutes): Your best material, filmed professionally. Not iPhone footage from a bar. This is what agents watch to decide if they'll represent you. The first 15 seconds determine if they keep watching.
  • Festival Submissions: Montreal Comedy Festival, Edinburgh Fringe, SXSW. Carter emphasizes: win or place at a major festival, and agents START calling you. Losing comedians still get agents if their demo reel is strong. Winning comedians get agents AND TV meetings.
  • Showcases: Industry events where agents/managers watch 50+ comedians in rapid succession. You get 5 minutes. If an agent likes you, they'll reach out after. These are NOT the same as open mics; they're professional cattle calls.
😂 FUNNY TAKE

"Your headshot is your resume. If it looks like a mugshot from a criminal investigation, agents will assume that's the role you play. Get a professional headshot. Your future HBO special will thank you."

🤖 AI CONNECTION

Comedians are optimizing for agent attention, just like machine learning models optimize for a loss function. Your demo reel, headshot, and festival wins are your "metrics." Agents evaluate these signals to decide if you're "worth the investment." The funnel (demo → showcase → agent → TV) is a ranking algorithm. You're trying to move up the leaderboard by improving your signals. LLMs do the same: they're evaluated on benchmarks, lose functions, and metrics that determine if they're "production-ready."

📚 CROSS-BOOK CONNECTION

Zero to One (Peter Thiel): Every business needs a unique positioning and distribution strategy. Comedy is no exception. Platform Revolution (Parker, Van Alstyne): Showcases and festivals are platforms that connect supply (comedians) with demand (agents/audiences).

"Your comedy career is a business. Treat your headshot, demo reel, and festival submissions like a product launch. Because they are."
— Judy Carter (paraphrased), The Comedy Bible

Comedy as Therapy

Turning pain into power — how comedians process trauma through humor and self-actualization

🩹 KEY LESSON

Comedy is a form of therapy and self-actualization. It's how comedians process trauma, reclaim agency, and turn pain into power. The comedian on stage is often more emotionally healthy than the audience member in the seat.

  • Processing vs. Venting: Venting is "I'm angry and I'm telling you why." Processing is "I'm angry, I examined why, and here's what I learned." Comedy requires processing. Hannah Gadsby's special wasn't just about trauma; it was about integrating trauma into a coherent worldview. That's why it landed so hard.
  • Reclaiming the Narrative: Trauma victims are often powerless. But when a comedian tells their trauma story on stage, they control the narrative. They decide the punchlines, the timing, the message. That reclamation is deeply healing. Patton Oswalt turned his wife's death into material that gave other grieving people permission to laugh.
  • Connection Through Vulnerability: The "tears of a clown" myth says comedians are sad inside. Carter reframes: comedians are humans who found a way to transform sadness into wisdom and wisdom into connection. That's not a pathology; that's enlightenment.
😂 FUNNY TAKE

"Your therapist charges $200/hour to listen to your trauma. Your comedy audience pays you $20 to laugh at it and feel less alone. One of you is fundamentally misunderstanding the value proposition."

🤖 AI CONNECTION

In machine learning, adversarial training (where a model is exposed to challenging examples and learns to handle them better) mirrors Carter's insight that trauma processed through comedy becomes strength. The model doesn't ignore hard examples; it integrates them. Similarly, comedians don't ignore pain; they integrate it into their voice. Transforming adversarial inputs (pain) into coherent outputs (comedy) is a form of learning and growth.

📚 CROSS-BOOK CONNECTION

Man's Search for Meaning (Viktor Frankl): Finding meaning in suffering is redemptive. Dare to Lead (Brené Brown): Vulnerability + witnessed acceptance = healing. Emotional Intelligence (Daniel Goleman): Processing emotions (not suppressing them) is what healthy people do.

"Comedy is how we transform what happened TO us into what we do WITH our lives. That's not entertainment. That's alchemy."
— Judy Carter (paraphrased), The Comedy Bible

Story Spotlight: From Magic to Comedy

Judy Carter's Transformation

Judy Carter started as a stage magician in the 1980s. She was good — booked at corporate events, performing illusions that dazzled audiences. But something felt hollow. The magic was impressive, but it wasn't connecting with people on a human level.

One day, after a failed illusion where the reveal didn't work, she ad-libbed a joke to save the act. The audience laughed. Harder than they'd ever laughed at the perfect tricks. That moment changed everything. She realized: people don't remember illusions. They remember how you made them feel, and how you made them laugh.

Carter started taking standup classes, learning joke structure, and studying comedians instead of magicians. Her magic background gave her physicality and stagecraft that most comedians lack. Her new comedy background gave her vulnerability and truth. The combination was explosive. Within five years, she was headlining clubs and teaching comedy workshops at UCLA.

The Comedy Workshop was born from this insight: if comedy can be taught deliberately (and she learned it, transitioning from another performance discipline), then anyone can learn it. This conviction became the foundation of "The Comedy Bible."

Story Spotlight: The UCLA Comedy Workshop

Transforming Students Into Comedians

At UCLA, Judy Carter developed a rigorous, structured approach to teaching comedy. Her students ranged from surgeons to lawyers to corporate executives — people with zero performance experience but strong personalities and interesting perspectives.

Within 12 weeks, they had 5-minute sets they could perform at open mics. Within 6 months, some were getting booked at clubs. The breakthrough: Carter didn't teach them to be funny. She taught them to be observant, intentional, and disciplined. Funny emerged as a byproduct.

One famous story: a cardiac surgeon in her class was initially terrified of performing. He was analytical, precise, but emotionally closed off — the opposite of a comedian. Carter pushed him to share stories about his career, his failures, his awkwardness in social situations. He wrote about the absurdity of telling a patient their heart is failing while maintaining professional composure. The specificity and truth made it devastatingly funny. He eventually performed at a club and crushed. He didn't become a comedian, but he discovered a powerful form of self-expression.

This story exemplifies Carter's core belief: comedy isn't a gift. It's a skill. And skills can be taught to anyone willing to do the work.

Story Spotlight: The Breakthrough Student

From Silent to Stellar

One student in Carter's workshop was a quiet, introverted woman who had never performed anything in her life. She was terrified of being the center of attention. Her voice was soft, her stage presence nonexistent.

Carter made her start with morning pages, observation journals, and daily joke writing. For weeks, she had nothing — just rambling thoughts and half-formed observations. No breakthroughs. But she kept showing up, kept writing.

Then it happened: she wrote a joke about her mother's unsolicited advice on dating. A simple observation: "My mom gives dating advice like she's still married to my dad. Her last relationship ended decades ago, but her opinions are FRESH." She delivered it with a tiny shrug and dead-pan expression. The class erupted.

That one joke unlocked everything. She realized she had a unique perspective (daughter of divorced parents navigating love). She had a specific attitude (bemused, slightly exasperated). She had physicality (the shrug). She developed a set around family, relationships, and growing up in between two worlds. She started performing at open mics, then clubs. Within two years, she had an agent meeting.

This story illustrates Carter's philosophy: everyone has comedy inside. Sometimes it takes a while to find it. But with structure, consistency, and the right prompts, it emerges.

Hard Truth + Attitude = Joke

  • Hard Truth: something real, painful, or absurd
  • Attitude: WEIRD, SCARY, HARD, or STUPID
  • Result: Comedy that lands because it's TRUE

  • 3 pages: Morning Pages (unfiltered writing)
  • 10 min: Observation Journal (capture "what bugs you?")

  • ⚡ WEIRD: Absurd, surreal, unexpected angle
  • 😨 SCARY: Threatening, dangerous, fearful
  • 💪 HARD: Difficult, unfair, grueling
  • 🤦 STUPID: Foolish, self-deprecating, absurd

📺 Sitcom Structure (22 min)

  • Cold Open (2 min): Standalone laugh

  • Act B (10 min): Escalate chaos
  • Tag (2 min): Button joke

  • A-Story (0:30-3:00): Main bit
  • Callbacks (3:00-4:30): Revisit setup
  • Out (4:30-5:00): Biggest laugh
