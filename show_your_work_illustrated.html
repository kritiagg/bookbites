Show Your Work!

Austin Kleon
10 ways to share your creativity and get discovered — translated for the creator who wants to grow big on Instagram, YouTube, comedy, and life (without becoming insufferable).
The 10 Ways · Scroll to begin
1

You Don't Have to Be a Genius

Forget the lone genius. Be a generous node in a "scenius."

🔑 Key Lesson

Kleon borrows Brian Eno's word "scenius" — great ideas don't come from solitary geniuses, they bubble up from a scene: a network of people stealing, copying, riffing, and cheering each other on. You don't need to be brilliant. You need to be a generous, contributing node. Be an amateur in the original sense (French: "lover") — someone obsessed enough to think out loud in public.

  • The internet itself is a giant scenius — Kleon's whole career grew from blackout poems he posted on a blog, not from a gallery anointing him.
  • YouTube creators in the same niche who collab, react, and shout each other out (the early gaming and beauty "scenes") grew far faster than loners — the rising tide lifted everyone.
  • Open-mic comedy scenes work exactly this way: comics steal rhythms, test bits on each other, and the whole room gets funnier together. Nobody invents stand-up alone in a cave.
😏 FUNNY TAKE

"Lone genius" is just "I have no friends and bad WiFi" with better PR. Nobody is sitting alone inventing comedy from scratch — you're standing on the shoulders of 400 people who already did the bit, you just added a better camera and a face filter. Contribute to the group chat, you parasite. Lovingly.

🤖 AI / ML CONNECTION

"Be a node, not a genius" is literally graph theory. A network's value isn't in any single vertex but in its centrality and connectivity — high-degree, well-connected nodes spread information fastest (PageRank rewards who links to you). And no LLM is a lone genius: it's trained on the collective "scenius" of the entire internet. Even the model is an amateur that read everyone else's homework.

🔗 CONNECTS TO

Steal Like an Artist (Kleon's prequel): nothing is original, so collect and remix. Contagious (Berger): ideas spread through social networks, not lone broadcasts — you want to be a connected node.

"Being a valuable part of a scenius is not about how smart or talented you are, but about what you have to contribute — the ideas you share, the quality of the connections you make, and the conversations you start."

— Austin Kleon, Show Your Work!

2

Think Process, Not Product

The finished thing is one post. The journey is a thousand.

🔑 Key Lesson

Audiences crave the process — the messy in-between. By becoming a documentarian of what you do, you turn the dull stretch between projects into content. You don't have to be making masterpieces daily; you just have to show the work. "Document, don't create" is the lazy creator's superpower.

  • Bon Appétit's Test Kitchen exploded by filming the mistakes, re-takes, and arguments — the cooking process, not glossy finished plates.
  • Casey Neistat's daily vlogs made the behind-the-scenes the product — millions watched him edit, fail, and ride a Boosted board to work.
  • Comedians posting "tag yourself in the audience" bombing clips or bit-development reels get more reach than a flawless special drop, because watching someone build is irresistible.
😏 FUNNY TAKE

Great news for the chronically unfinished: your half-baked nonsense is now "content." That blooper where you forgot the punchline? Post it. The 19 takes of you saying "wait, from the top"? A reel. "Process over product" is divine permission to monetize your inability to ever finish anything. You're not a procrastinator, you're a documentarian.

🤖 AI / ML CONNECTION

Sharing your process is training transparency. The product is the final model checkpoint; the process is the loss curves, ablations, and failed runs — and that's what the field actually learns from. Model cards and "reasoning traces" exist precisely because showing how you got there is more useful than the polished output alone. Show your gradients, not just your final weights.

🔗 CONNECTS TO

Atomic Habits (Clear): fall in love with systems, not goals — process > product, exactly. The War of Art (Pressfield): the work is the daily turning-up, not the trophy. Document the turning-up.

"Once you start looking at the world this way, you'll start to see that you have something to share every day, even if you don't think you're an expert: the process."

— Austin Kleon, Show Your Work!

3

Share Something Small Every Day

The "daily dispatch" beats a résumé. Tiny, consistent, public.

🔑 Key Lesson

Don't wait for the masterpiece. Send a daily dispatch — one small, generous bit of media (a tip, a clip, a sketch, a thought). A daily dispatch beats a résumé because it shows what you're working on right now. Ask at day's end: "What did I work on? What's worth sharing?" Then post that.

  • The algorithm rewards frequency: Instagram and YouTube both surface accounts that feed the machine consistently — a steady stream of small posts trains the recommendation system on who you reach.
  • MrBeast's early years were thousands of small, unglamorous uploads — he obsessively iterated daily long before any video popped.
  • Comedians on TikTok who post one bit per day out-grow the one who drops a "perfect" video monthly — reps beat polish, and one of the small ones eventually catches fire.
😏 FUNNY TAKE

"Post something small every day" is how you find out you contain roughly 11 days of personality before you're recycling the same opinion about airport behavior. That's fine. The bar is not "viral genius," the bar is "exists daily." Your competition also ran out of ideas — they're just braver about posting the bad ones.

🤖 AI / ML CONNECTION

Daily posting is iterative gradient descent. You don't leap to the optimum in one giant heroic step — you take many small steps, each nudged by feedback (likes, comments, the loss signal). Big infrequent updates overshoot and destabilize; small frequent ones converge. Your posting cadence is your learning rate. Ship small, learn the gradient, adjust.

🔗 CONNECTS TO

Atomic Habits (Clear): 1% better daily, the power of small consistent reps and never missing twice. The War of Art (Pressfield): the pro shows up every day regardless of mood — the daily dispatch is turning pro.

"A daily dispatch is even better than a résumé, because it shows what we're working on right now."

— Austin Kleon, Show Your Work!

4

Open Up Your Cabinet of Curiosities

Share your influences. Good theft is generous attribution.

🔑 Key Lesson

Share what inspires you — your influences, your "cabinet of curiosities." Don't hide your sources; credit them generously. People are as interested in your taste as your work, and crediting others connects you to their audiences. If you steal, credit loudly and link back — bad theft hides the source; good theft pays it forward.

  • Tarantino openly cites the kung-fu and grindhouse films he remixes — naming influences made him an icon, not a thief.
  • "Get Ready With Me" + "my influences" creators grow by sharing the products, books, and accounts they love — recommendation is its own content genre.
  • Comedians naming who shaped them (a Carlin riff, a Mitch Hedberg cadence) earn respect and tap fans of those legends, rather than pretending they invented the form.
😏 FUNNY TAKE

Listing your influences is how you preemptively tell the comment section "yes I know I sound like him." It's cheaper than a lawyer and more flattering than getting caught. Credit your heroes loudly — partly because it's right, mostly because their fans will follow you out of curiosity, and a borrowed audience is still an audience.

🤖 AI / ML CONNECTION

Opening your cabinet of curiosities is publishing your training data and citations. Open-source datasets and dataset cards exist so the world can see what fed the model. Attribution and provenance — knowing which sources shaped an output — is the whole frontier of responsible AI. Your influence list is your bibliography; a model's is its data lineage.

🔗 CONNECTS TO

Steal Like an Artist (Kleon): collect, remix, and credit your "creative lineage." Contagious (Berger): "Social Currency" — sharing cool finds makes you look like an insider, which is why curation spreads.

"Your influences are all worth sharing because they clue people in to who you are and what you do — sometimes even more than your own work."

— Austin Kleon, Show Your Work!

5

Tell Good Stories

The work doesn't speak for itself. Give it a narrative.

🔑 Key Lesson

Work doesn't speak for itself — context does. Learn to tell the story behind it: where it came from, what you struggled with, why it matters. A good "About" page, a tight origin story, and a clear narrative arc make people care. Master the structure (setup → conflict → resolution) and know your audience.

  • Humans of New York turned ordinary strangers into riveting feeds purely through the story attached to each photo.
  • The best YouTube thumbnails + titles are tiny stories that create a curiosity gap — the video is the resolution you click for.
  • Stand-up itself is structure: a great comedian sets up a relatable premise, escalates the conflict, and lands the resolution (the punchline) — story mechanics in miniature.
😏 FUNNY TAKE

"The work speaks for itself" is what people say right before nobody listens to the work. Your masterpiece is mute; it needs a hype-man, and that hype-man is you, badly, in a caption. Every creator eventually learns that "I made this on a hard week" gets ten times the engagement of the actual thing you made. The arc, not the art.

🤖 AI / ML CONNECTION

"Tell good stories" is structured context / prompting. Raw data points mean nothing to a model without sequence and framing — narrative is how you arrange tokens so meaning emerges. Transformers literally model the probability of what comes next; a coherent story is a low-perplexity, high-signal sequence. Good narrative = well-conditioned context window.

🔗 CONNECTS TO

Hook Point (Kane): a 3-second hook is a story's opening gambit — the curiosity gap that earns the next second. Contagious (Berger): "Stories" is one of his six STEPPS — ideas travel wrapped in narrative Trojan horses.

"Words matter. Artists love to trot out the tired line, 'My work speaks for itself,' but the truth is, our work does not speak for itself."

— Austin Kleon, Show Your Work!

6

Teach What You Know

Give away your secrets. Teaching builds an audience and bonds.

🔑 Key Lesson

The moment you learn something, teach it. Sharing your knowledge — recipes, code, techniques, "how I did this" breakdowns — doesn't diminish you; it builds trust, an audience, and a bond. Teaching also forces you to understand your own work better. Giving away your "secrets" makes people more invested in you, not less.

  • Tutorial creators (cooking, Photoshop, coding) built entire careers by giving the recipe away — the goodwill returns as a loyal following.
  • Ali Abdaal grew a massive channel teaching study and productivity techniques he was figuring out himself in real time.
  • Comedians who break down "how a joke works" (writing podcasts, bit-dissection videos) earn die-hard fans who feel let in on the craft — and that intimacy converts to ticket sales.
😏 FUNNY TAKE

"Won't teaching my secrets create competitors?" Babe, nobody is going to out-you-you. People watch a 12-minute tutorial, feel deeply inspired, and then immediately go take a nap. You are selling the fantasy of doing the thing, which is a far bigger market than the people who actually do it. Give away the recipe; you're still the only restaurant they trust.

🤖 AI / ML CONNECTION

Teaching what you know is knowledge distillation. A larger "teacher" model transfers its know-how to a smaller "student," and — crucially — the student often generalizes better than if trained from raw data alone. Explaining forces compression of your knowledge into transferable form, exactly like distilling soft labels. Teach to distill; distill to clarify your own weights.

🔗 CONNECTS TO

Contagious (Berger): "Practical Value" — useful, teachable content gets shared because helping others is social currency. Atomic Habits (Clear): teaching cements your own identity ("I am someone who knows this"), reinforcing the habit loop.

"Teaching people doesn't subtract value from what you do, it actually adds to it. When you teach someone how to do your work, you are, in effect, generating more interest in your work."

— Austin Kleon, Show Your Work!

7

Don't Turn Into Human Spam

Be findable, not annoying. Quality of connection > quantity.

🔑 Key Lesson

If you want followers, be someone worth following — and that means giving more than you take. The opposite is "human spam": the person who only self-promotes, mass-DMs "check out my page," and treats every connection as a transaction. Be open, generous, and a good citizen of the scene. Don't be a vampire who drains attention without giving any back.

  • Kleon's "vampire test": avoid people (and behaviors) that leave you and others drained — and don't be that person to your audience.
  • The "engagement pod" comment-spammer ("Great post! Follow me back 🔥") fools no algorithm and repels every human — the opposite of building a scene.
  • Comedians who reply, support other openers, and amplify peers get booked and championed; the one who only plugs their own show in every reply gets quietly muted.
😏 FUNNY TAKE

There is a special circle of social-media hell for the person who DMs "Hey! 😊" and follows it 0.4 seconds later with a Linktree. We all know you didn't want to know how I'm doing. The math is brutal: every "check out my page 🔥" comment is a tiny withdrawal from a bank account of goodwill you never deposited into. Give first. Beg never.

🤖 AI / ML CONNECTION

Human spam is low-quality signal that gets penalized. Spam classifiers and recommender systems down-rank repetitive, self-serving, low-engagement behavior — and platforms model "authentic engagement" as a feature. Flooding output with the same promo is like overfitting to one token: high volume, near-zero information gain. Be high-signal; the ranker is literally trained to detect spam.

🔗 CONNECTS TO

Contagious (Berger): "Social Currency" — people share what makes them look good, never your desperate plug. Hook Point (Kane): stand out with value, not volume — being interesting beats being loud.

"If you want fans, you have to be a fan first. If you want to be accepted by a community, you have to first be a good citizen of that community."

— Austin Kleon, Show Your Work!

8

Learn to Take a Punch

Put work out, get hit, build a thicker skin. Criticism ≠ death.

🔑 Key Lesson

Putting work in public means getting hit. Kleon says learn to take a punch: toughen up, protect your vulnerable spots, and roll with the blows. Wait to respond, keep work flowing (a body of work cushions any single hit), and learn to tell the difference between useful criticism and noise. Block trolls without guilt; mine real feedback for gold.

  • The "don't read the comments" rule exists because creators learned the hard way that the loudest critics are rarely the target audience.
  • YouTubers who post a "responding to my haters" video turn criticism into content and disarm it — taking the punch publicly and staying standing.
  • Every comedian bombs. The pros treat a dead room as data ("that bit needs work"), not a verdict on their soul — they're back on stage the next night.
😏 FUNNY TAKE

Nothing prepares you for a stranger named xX_dragonslayer_Xx with a profile pic of a cartoon frog deciding your life's work is "mid." But here's the secret: a hater watched your whole video to comment that. That's a view. That's watch time. Thank them in your heart and block them with your hand. The algorithm doesn't know "ratio'd" from "beloved" — it only counts that you stayed in the ring.

🤖 AI / ML CONNECTION

Taking a punch is robustness to adversarial inputs and noisy labels. A brittle model collapses on one weird example; a robust one (trained with adversarial examples and regularization) absorbs the hit and generalizes. Criticism is your validation signal — but you must filter noisy labels (trolls) from true error signal (real feedback), or you'll overfit to the loudest, least representative data point.

🔗 CONNECTS TO

The War of Art (Pressfield): Resistance attacks hardest when you go public — the pro endures criticism without ego collapse. Atomic Habits (Clear): identity-based resilience — one bad day doesn't break who you are.

"Once you put your work out into the world, you have to be prepared for the good, the bad, and the ugly... The more criticism you take, the more you realize it can't hurt you."

— Austin Kleon, Show Your Work!

9

Sell Out

It's okay to make money. Charge. Build a mailing list. Survive.

🔑 Key Lesson

"Selling out" isn't shameful — it's how you keep making the work. Don't be afraid to charge for value or ask for support. Kleon's biggest practical tip: build an email list — it's the one audience you truly own, unlike followers you rent from a platform that can change the rules overnight. The "ask" is okay; just keep giving away enough that the ask feels earned.

  • Patreon / memberships: fans want to support creators they love — letting them pay is a service, not a betrayal.
  • Email lists survive algorithm changes: when reach gets throttled, the creators who own an email list still reach 100% of subscribers.
  • Comedians selling merch and tickets directly turn online attention into rent money — the free clips are the funnel, the live show is the business.
😏 FUNNY TAKE

"I don't do it for the money" is a beautiful sentiment usually said by people who have money. Charging is not selling out; starving is not artistic integrity, it's just being broke with extra steps. And please, for the love of the scenius: build an email list. Followers are a flock of birds that will leave the second the algorithm sneezes. An email list is the one fence the platform can't tear down.

🤖 AI / ML CONNECTION

Owning your email list is owning your distribution and your data pipeline instead of renting it from a black-box recommender you can't see or control. Platform algorithms are a non-stationary environment — the reward function shifts under you without notice. An owned channel is a stable, direct connection to your true distribution: no intermediary model deciding whether your "request" gets served.

🔗 CONNECTS TO

The War of Art (Pressfield): "turning pro" means treating your craft as a livelihood, money included. Hook Point (Kane): attention is only valuable if you convert it — own the channel that lets you do so.

"Of all the social networks that have sprung up, the most powerful one is still email... Don't waste your time worrying about being a sellout."

— Austin Kleon, Show Your Work!

10

Stick Around

Don't quit. Endurance is the rarest superpower in a feed full of flameouts.

🔑 Key Lesson

Most people quit. Sticking around — for years, through plateaus and dry spells — is itself a massive competitive advantage. After finishing one project, don't bask or panic; start the next. Kleon advises taking sabbaticals, going on "creative detox," and avoiding burnout so you can play the long game. Don't quit your show when it's working; reinvent and keep going. Chain your projects so the end of one is the start of the next.

  • The 10-year overnight success: nearly every "sudden" creator (and Kleon himself) posted in obscurity for years before the breakthrough.
  • Channels that survive a viral lull by simply continuing to post outlast the one-hit accounts that quit when the spike faded.
  • Comedy careers are decades long: the comics you admire grinded open mics for 10+ years — longevity, not a single great set, built the name.
😏 FUNNY TAKE

Most of your "competition" will quit by Tuesday. Genuinely. The bar for "sticking around" is so low you could win the long game by simply continuing to exist while everyone else rage-deletes their account after a flop. Going viral once is luck. Going viral once a year for ten years is a career — and it's mostly just refusing to leave the party after the snacks run out.

🤖 AI / ML CONNECTION

Sticking around is surviving the loss plateau without early stopping at the wrong epoch. Training curves flatten for long stretches before a phase change ("grokking") where generalization suddenly clicks. Quit during the plateau and you never reach it. Endurance = enough training steps for the model to break through; the creators who flame out stopped one epoch before the loss dropped.

🔗 CONNECTS TO

Atomic Habits (Clear): the "plateau of latent potential" — results lag effort, so stay in the game. The War of Art (Pressfield): the pro shows up for life; Resistance hopes you'll quit right before the breakthrough.

"Don't quit your show. You can't plan on anything; you can only stay open and prepared to seize the opportunities as they come."

— Austin Kleon, Show Your Work!

Spotlight · The Origin of "Show Your Work"

Austin Kleon Built a Career in Public

Kleon didn't start as a famous author. He started by posting "newspaper blackout poems" — poems made by crossing out words in a newspaper page — to his blog, in public, for free. He showed the process, shared something small repeatedly, and connected with a scene of other writers and artists online.

That practice of working in public grew into a book deal, a bestselling trilogy (Steal Like an Artist, Show Your Work!, Keep Going), and a long-running newsletter. He is, quite literally, the case study for his own advice: he didn't wait to be a genius — he became a generous node in a scenius, posted daily, and stuck around for years.

Spotlight · The Big Idea Underneath It All

Be an Amateur on Purpose

Kleon's most liberating idea: in a world of experts, the amateur wins by being unafraid. Amateurs (from the Latin/French for "lover") are enthusiasts who pursue the work for love, share their enthusiasm openly, and aren't paralyzed by the fear of looking stupid. That fearlessness is exactly what makes them magnetic — and shareable.

The professional often hoards knowledge and plays defense to protect a reputation. The amateur learns in public, makes mistakes out loud, and invites everyone along. For a creator chasing growth, this is the cheat code: your beginner energy is content, your enthusiasm is contagious, and "figuring it out live" is the most relatable thing on the internet.
