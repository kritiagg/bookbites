Learning by Trial, Error, and Reward

The agent–environment loop and the Markov Decision Process (MDP)

The loop that defines all of RL: act, observe, get rewarded, repeat.
◆ Key idea

Reinforcement learning studies an agent that learns to act inside an environment to maximize cumulative reward. Nobody hands it labeled examples of "the right move." It tries things, sees what reward comes back, and gradually shifts toward behavior that pays off. This is the formalization of learning from consequences.

Almost every RL problem is modeled as a Markov Decision Process, a 5-tuple \((\mathcal{S},\mathcal{A},P,R,\gamma)\):

  • States \(\mathcal{S}\) — the situations the agent can be in.
  • Actions \(\mathcal{A}\) — what it can do.
  • Transitions \(P(s'\mid s,a)\) — the world's dynamics: probability of landing in \(s'\).
  • Reward \(R(s,a)\) — scalar feedback for acting.
  • Discount \(\gamma\in[0,1]\) — how much the future counts.

The agent's behavior is a policy \(\pi(a\mid s)\): a rule (possibly random) mapping states to actions. RL is the search for a policy that collects the most reward over time.

The Markov property
\[ P(s_{t+1}\mid s_t,a_t,s_{t-1},a_{t-1},\dots) = P(s_{t+1}\mid s_t,a_t) \]
Intuition

"Markov" means the present state is a sufficient summary of the past — to predict the future, you only need where you are now, not the whole history of how you got here. A well-designed state makes this true. Chess is Markov (the board is everything); "am I winning the conversation?" usually is not, which is why state design is half the battle.

😅 Funny take

RL is just training a dog, except the dog has no idea what "sit" means, gets treats on a 12-step delay, and occasionally you forgot to define what a treat even is. Then you act surprised when it learns to knock over the treat jar instead.

🤖 AI connection

An LLM being aligned with RLHF is exactly this loop: the state is the prompt-so-far, the action is the next token (or the whole completion), the policy is the model \(\pi_\theta\), and the reward comes from a learned reward model standing in for human preferences. Every later chapter builds toward that mapping.

🔗 Cross-guide connection

See The Alignment Problem for why reward design is dangerous, and Thinking, Fast and Slow — the agent's "fast" habit (policy) vs. "slow" planning (value iteration) mirror System 1 and System 2.

"Reinforcement learning is learning what to do — how to map situations to actions — so as to maximize a numerical reward signal."
— Sutton & Barto, Reinforcement Learning: An Introduction

The Goal Is the Return, Not the Next Reward

Why we sum rewards over time, and why we shrink the future

Discounting turns an infinite future into a finite, well-behaved sum.
◆ Key idea

A greedy agent that grabs the biggest immediate reward is usually a bad agent — think of skipping study to watch TV. What we actually maximize is the return \(G_t\): the (discounted) sum of all future rewards from time \(t\).

Discounted return
\[ G_t = r_{t} + \gamma r_{t+1} + \gamma^2 r_{t+2} + \dots = \sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\gamma^{k}\,r_{t+k} \]

The discount factor \(\gamma\) does three jobs at once: (1) it makes the infinite sum converge to something finite (bounded by \(r_{\max}/(1-\gamma)\)); (2) it encodes impatience — a reward now beats the same reward later; (3) it hedges uncertainty about a distant, hard-to-predict future. \(\gamma=0.99\) means you care roughly 100 steps ahead; \(\gamma=0\) means pure greed.

Intuition

\(\gamma\) is an interest rate run backwards. Money you'll get in 10 years is worth less than money today; a reward 10 steps away is "discounted" the same way. Push \(\gamma\) toward 1 and the agent becomes a patient long-term planner; drop it and it becomes a mayfly chasing the nearest treat.

⚠ Pitfall

Reward is not the goal you have in your head — it's the number you literally wrote down. Agents ruthlessly maximize the written number. A boat-racing agent famously learned to spin in circles hitting bonus targets forever instead of finishing the race. This is reward hacking, and it haunts RLHF too.

😅 Funny take

Setting \(\gamma\) is choosing your agent's personality on a slider from "toddler who wants the cookie NOW" to "monk planning for a reward in the next life." Most working agents are set to "anxious grad student": cares about the next few years, has quietly given up on the far future.

🤖 AI connection

In RLHF for LLMs the horizon is short — often the reward lands once, at the end of the completion. That makes it closer to a contextual bandit (one decision, one reward) than a long MDP, which is exactly why the policy-gradient math simplifies so cleanly in the derivation you were reading.

"The reward hypothesis: all of what we mean by goals and purposes can be thought of as the maximization of the expected value of the cumulative sum of a received scalar signal."
— Richard Sutton

How Good Is This State? The Value Functions

V(s), Q(s,a), and the advantage that connects them

V asks about a state; Q about a state–action pair; A about how much better one action is than average.
◆ Key idea

A value function answers "starting here and following policy \(\pi\), how much return should I expect?" Two flavors:

State-value and action-value
\[ V^{\pi}(s) = \mathbb{E}_{\pi}\!\left[G_t \mid s_t = s\right] \qquad Q^{\pi}(s,a) = \mathbb{E}_{\pi}\!\left[G_t \mid s_t=s,\,a_t=a\right] \]

\(V^\pi(s)\) grades a state under your current habits. \(Q^\pi(s,a)\) grades a specific action in that state, then reverts to policy \(\pi\). They're linked by averaging over the policy's actions:

\[ V^{\pi}(s) = \sum_a \pi(a\mid s)\,Q^{\pi}(s,a) \]

The difference is the advantage, the single most useful quantity in modern policy methods:

Advantage
\[ A^{\pi}(s,a) = Q^{\pi}(s,a) - V^{\pi}(s) \]
Intuition

\(V\) is your credit score for a situation. \(Q\) is the score for a situation plus a specific first move. Advantage \(A\) is "did this move beat my own baseline?" — positive means better than usual, negative means worse. If you only remember one thing: reinforce actions with positive advantage. That's the heartbeat of actor-critic and PPO.

😅 Funny take

\(V(s)\) is Zillow estimating your house's worth assuming you keep living your normal life. \(Q(s,a)\) is Zillow's estimate if you first knock out a wall. Advantage is your contractor gently telling you whether the wall was a good idea.

🤖 AI connection

In PPO-based RLHF a value head is bolted onto the transformer to estimate \(V(s)\) at each token. Subtracting it as a baseline is what makes the noisy reward signal usable — without it, gradients are far too high-variance to train a billion-parameter policy.

🔗 Cross-guide connection

Compare Thinking in Bets — Annie Duke's "was it a good decision or just a good outcome?" is precisely the \(Q\)-vs-realized-return distinction. Value functions separate decision quality from luck.

"Almost all reinforcement learning algorithms involve estimating value functions."
— Sutton & Barto

The Bellman Equation: Value Talks to Its Future Self

The recursion that every RL algorithm secretly solves

One step of look-ahead: a value equals immediate reward plus discounted value of where you land.
◆ Key idea

Richard Bellman's insight (1957): the return has a recursive shape. Everything after the first step is just another return, starting one step later. Split the sum and you get the Bellman expectation equation:

Bellman expectation (for a fixed policy π)
\[ V^{\pi}(s) = \sum_a \pi(a\mid s)\sum_{s'} P(s'\mid s,a)\Big[R(s,a) + \gamma V^{\pi}(s')\Big] \]

To act optimally, replace "average over the policy" with "take the best action." That gives the Bellman optimality equation, whose solution \(V^*\) / \(Q^*\) defines the best possible behavior:

Bellman optimality
\[ Q^{*}(s,a) = R(s,a) + \gamma \sum_{s'} P(s'\mid s,a)\,\max_{a'} Q^{*}(s',a') \]

The greedy policy on \(Q^*\) — always pick \(\arg\max_a Q^*(s,a)\) — is an optimal policy. Solve this equation and you have solved the MDP.

Intuition

"The value of where I am equals the reward I get now, plus the (discounted) value of where I'll be next." It's dynamic programming's core trick: a giant problem (value over an infinite future) collapses into a relationship between neighbors. Solve the local consistency everywhere and the global answer falls out.

😅 Funny take

The Bellman equation is the ultimate "I'll deal with it tomorrow" — except it actually works, because tomorrow-you is also running a Bellman equation and passing the buck to the day after. Infinite procrastination that somehow converges to optimal.

🤖 AI connection

Q-learning and DQN are nothing but iteratively enforcing the Bellman optimality equation: the "TD error" you'll meet next chapter is literally the two sides of this equation failing to match, and learning nudges them together. The Bellman equation is the target your neural network chases.

🔗 Cross-guide connection

The recursive "solve small overlapping subproblems" spirit echoes Gödel, Escher, Bach and its self-referential loops — value defined in terms of itself, yet perfectly well-posed.

"An optimal policy has the property that whatever the initial state and initial decision are, the remaining decisions must constitute an optimal policy with regard to the state resulting from the first decision."
— Richard Bellman, the Principle of Optimality

When You Know the Rules: Dynamic Programming

Policy evaluation, policy iteration, value iteration

Policy iteration: evaluate, then improve, then repeat — a two-step dance that provably reaches the optimum.
◆ Key idea

If you know the model (\(P\) and \(R\)), you can solve the MDP by turning the Bellman equations into update rules. Two classic algorithms:

Policy iteration alternates two steps until the policy stops changing: (1) policy evaluation — sweep the Bellman expectation update until \(V^\pi\) settles; (2) policy improvement — set \(\pi(s)=\arg\max_a Q^\pi(s,a)\). Each round is guaranteed no worse than the last, and it converges to \(\pi^*\) in finite steps.

Value iteration skips full evaluation and just hammers the Bellman optimality update directly:

Value iteration update
\[ V_{k+1}(s) \leftarrow \max_a \Big[R(s,a) + \gamma \sum_{s'}P(s'\mid s,a)\,V_k(s')\Big] \]

This is a contraction mapping: each sweep shrinks the error by a factor of \(\gamma\), so \(V_k \to V^*\) geometrically. The catch: you must sweep every state, which is impossible when states are astronomically many (or continuous). That limitation is the reason the rest of this book exists.

Intuition

Value iteration is rumor propagation. At first only states next to a reward "know" they're valuable. Each sweep, the good news spreads one step further out, discounted by \(\gamma\). After enough sweeps every state has heard how far it is from reward and how to head toward it.

😅 Funny take

Dynamic programming is the honor-roll student who reads the entire textbook, including the appendix, before the exam. Flawless — until the textbook is 10¹⁷⁰ pages long (that's Go), at which point they quietly transfer into the "learn from experience" program in Chapter 6.

🤖 AI connection

Pure DP is rarely used on big AI problems because it needs the model and a full state sweep. But its fingerprint is everywhere: bootstrapping (updating a value estimate from other value estimates) is the DP idea that survives into TD learning, Q-learning, and the value heads inside RLHF.

🔗 Cross-guide connection

Thinking, Fast and Slow: DP is pure "System 2" — deliberate, exhaustive planning. Model-free RL (next) is "System 1" — cached intuition built from repetition. Real agents blend both.

"I decided to call it dynamic programming… it was something not even a Congressman could object to."
— Richard Bellman, on naming his method to survive a budget-hostile boss

Learning From Experience: Monte Carlo & TD

The temporal-difference error — RL's most important signal

MC learns from complete, real returns; TD bootstraps from its own next-step guess — and learns online.
◆ Key idea

Drop the assumption that you know the model. Now you must learn from sampled experience. Two styles of estimating \(V^\pi\):

Monte Carlo (MC): play a full episode, observe the actual return \(G_t\), then nudge your estimate toward it: \(V(s_t)\leftarrow V(s_t)+\alpha\,[G_t - V(s_t)]\). Unbiased, but you must wait for the episode to end and it's high-variance.

Temporal Difference (TD): don't wait. After one step, use the reward plus your own current guess of the next state's value as the target. The gap between prediction and this one-step target is the TD error \(\delta_t\):

TD(0) update and the TD error
\[ \delta_t = \underbrace{r_{t+1} + \gamma V(s_{t+1})}_{\text{target (guess)}} - V(s_t), \qquad V(s_t) \leftarrow V(s_t) + \alpha\,\delta_t \]

TD bootstraps — it learns a guess from a guess — trading a little bias for much lower variance and the ability to learn online, step by step, before an episode ever finishes.

Intuition

MC is grading yourself only after the final exam, using your true score. TD is updating your self-assessment after every question: "that felt better than expected — bump my confidence a bit." The TD error is that little jolt of "better/worse than I thought," and it is, strikingly, the same signal dopamine neurons fire in the brain.

😅 Funny take

TD learning is judging a first date at each course instead of waiting to see if you get married. Riskier per-signal, but you find out about the "chews with mouth open" TD error before you've committed 40 years of discounted return.

🤖 AI connection

The TD error is the engine of nearly every value-based deep RL method. In actor-critic RLHF, the critic is trained by TD, and Generalized Advantage Estimation (GAE) — used in PPO — is literally an exponentially-weighted sum of TD errors \(\delta_t\). Learn TD and you've learned the substrate of the whole modern stack.

🔗 Cross-guide connection

The dopamine-as-prediction-error link runs straight through Dopamine Nation and Behave — your brain appears to run a TD-learning algorithm on reward.

"If one had to identify one idea as central and novel to reinforcement learning, it would undoubtedly be temporal-difference learning."
— Sutton & Barto

Q-Learning: Learning the Best Move Without a Map

On-policy (SARSA) vs off-policy (Q-learning), and exploration

The only difference is the target: the action you took (SARSA) vs the best possible action (Q-learning).
◆ Key idea

To control (not just evaluate), learn \(Q(s,a)\) from experience, then act greedily on it. Q-learning (Watkins, 1989) applies the Bellman optimality target:

Q-learning update
\[ Q(s_t,a_t) \leftarrow Q(s_t,a_t) + \alpha\Big[\,r_{t+1} + \gamma \max_{a'} Q(s_{t+1},a') - Q(s_t,a_t)\,\Big] \]

The \(\max\) makes it off-policy: it learns the value of the optimal policy even while you behave with a random, exploratory one. Swap that \(\max\) for the value of the action you actually took next and you get SARSA (on-policy), which learns the value of the policy it's really following — including its mistakes.

Exploration vs exploitation: you can't act greedily on \(Q\) you haven't learned yet. The standard fix is \(\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}\)-greedy: act greedily with probability \(1-\varepsilon\), pick a random action with probability \(\varepsilon\), and decay \(\varepsilon\) over time.

Intuition

Off-policy Q-learning is learning to drive optimally while a nervous instructor keeps jerking the wheel randomly (exploration). Because the update always asks "what's the best I could have done next?", it distills the ideal policy out of messy, exploratory data. SARSA instead learns "how good is my actual, jittery driving" — safer near cliffs, because it accounts for the random swerves it will really make.

⚠ Pitfall

The \(\max\) operator makes Q-learning over-optimistic: taking the max of noisy estimates systematically overestimates value (maximization bias). Double Q-learning — using one network to pick the action and another to score it — fixes this, and reappears as Double DQN.

😅 Funny take

ε-greedy is the "I'll usually get my usual, but 10% of the time I point at a random menu item and pray." Ninety percent of dinners are fine; the other 10% are how you discovered you actually love the weird dumplings. That's exploration paying rent.

🤖 AI connection

Q-learning's off-policy nature is a superpower for LLMs and robotics: you can learn from a replay buffer of old, logged, or human-generated data rather than only fresh on-policy rollouts. This is the seed of the deep-Q revolution in the next chapter, and of offline RL.

"Q-learning converges to the optimal action-values with probability 1, so long as all actions are repeatedly sampled in all states."
— Watkins & Dayan, 1992

Deep Q-Networks: When States Are Too Many to Count

Neural nets as value functions, experience replay, target networks

DQN maps raw pixels to a Q-value for every action in one forward pass.
◆ Key idea

A lookup table for \(Q(s,a)\) is hopeless when states are images or continuous. Replace the table with a function approximator \(Q_\theta(s,a)\) — a neural network. Train it to minimize the squared TD error against a Bellman target:

DQN loss
\[ L(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{(s,a,r,s')\sim \mathcal{D}}\Big[\big(\,r + \gamma \max_{a'} Q_{\theta^-}(s',a') - Q_\theta(s,a)\,\big)^2\Big] \]

DeepMind's DQN (2013/2015) cracked 49 Atari games from raw pixels with two stabilizing tricks that made "deep + RL" actually train:

  • Experience replay \(\mathcal{D}\): store transitions in a buffer and sample random minibatches. This breaks the temporal correlation between consecutive frames and reuses data.
  • Target network \(\theta^-\): compute the target with an old, frozen copy of the weights, updated only occasionally. Chasing a moving target you also control causes divergence; freezing it stabilizes learning.
Intuition

The deadly triad — function approximation + bootstrapping + off-policy learning — can make naive value RL blow up. DQN's two tricks are the guardrails: replay decorrelates the data so it looks more i.i.d., and the frozen target stops you from redecorating the goalposts every step. It's the difference between "learns Breakout" and "loss goes to infinity."

😅 Funny take

Training without a target network is trying to hit a dartboard that moves exactly when and where you throw, because you're holding it. Bolt the dartboard to the wall for a while (freeze \(\theta^-\)) and suddenly you can aim. Revolutionary idea: don't move the target.

🤖 AI connection

DQN was the "AlexNet moment" for RL — proof that gradient descent on a deep net could learn control end-to-end. Its descendants (Double DQN, Dueling, Prioritized Replay, Rainbow) refine the same core. And the value network in RLHF's PPO is a direct conceptual heir: a deep net predicting expected return.

🔗 Cross-guide connection

See Deep Learning (Goodfellow et al.) for the function-approximation machinery, and Attention Is All You Need for the transformer that becomes the policy in RLHF.

"The agent was able to surpass the performance of all previous algorithms and achieve a level comparable to a professional human games tester across 49 games, using the same algorithm, network architecture and hyperparameters."
— Mnih et al., Human-level control through deep RL, Nature 2015

Policy Gradients: Optimize the Behavior Directly

The REINFORCE derivation — the exact "deriving the rule" slide, line by line

Exactly the slide you shared: turn an un-samplable sum into a samplable expectation.
◆ Key idea

Value-based methods learn \(Q\) and act greedily. Policy-gradient methods skip that and optimize the policy \(\pi_\theta\) directly by gradient ascent on expected reward \(J(\theta)\). This shines when actions are continuous or the action space is enormous — like the vocabulary of a language model. Walking the four lines:

1 · Objective (expectation = π-weighted sum)
\[ J(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{y\sim\pi_\theta}[R(x,y)]=\sum_y \pi_\theta(y\mid x)\,R(x,y) \]
2 · Differentiate (R doesn't depend on θ)
\[ \nabla_\theta J(\theta)=\sum_y R(x,y)\,\nabla_\theta \pi_\theta(y\mid x) \]
3 · Log-derivative trick: since \(\nabla\pi=\pi\,\nabla\log\pi\)
\[ \nabla_\theta J(\theta)=\sum_y R(x,y)\,\pi_\theta(y\mid x)\,\nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y\mid x) \]
4 · Recognize the π-weighted sum as an expectation
\[ \nabla_\theta J(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{y\sim\pi_\theta}\!\big[R(x,y)\,\nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y\mid x)\big] \]

Line 2 is a sum over all completions — impossible. Line 4 is an expectation — so estimate it by sampling completions and averaging \(R\cdot\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta\). Take a step \(\Delta\theta\propto\) this estimate. That's REINFORCE (Williams, 1992).

Intuition

Read line 4 as: "increase the log-probability of each sampled completion, weighted by the reward it earned." High reward → push its probability up hard. Low (or negative) reward → push it down. Nothing about the environment's internals is needed — only the ability to sample and to compute \(\nabla\log\pi_\theta\), which is just a backward pass. The log-prob gradient is the "responsibility signal": which parameters made this exact output more likely?

⚠ Pitfall — variance

Raw REINFORCE is extremely high-variance: rewards are noisy and a single lucky episode yanks the gradient around. The fix is a baseline \(b(s)\): subtract any state-dependent quantity (it doesn't bias the gradient because \(\mathbb{E}[\nabla\log\pi]=0\)). Use \(b(s)=V(s)\) and the weight becomes the advantage \(A(s,a)=Q-V\) — reinforce only what beats the baseline. This single trick is why actor-critic exists.

😅 Funny take

REINFORCE without a baseline is a manager who gives raises based on total company revenue: everyone gets a bonus in a good quarter, even the guy who spent it playing solitaire. Subtracting the baseline (advantage) is finally asking "did you beat expectations?" — noisy performance reviews, now with signal.

🤖 AI connection

This is the RLHF gradient. For an LLM: \(x\) = prompt, \(y\) = sampled completion, \(\pi_\theta\) = the model, \(R(x,y)\) = a learned reward model's score. Because reward usually arrives once at the end, the long-horizon MDP collapses to this clean bandit-style rule — which is exactly why the slide teaches policy gradients through the LLM lens. PPO, DPO, and GRPO are all refinements of this one equation.

🔗 Cross-guide connection

The "sample and average to estimate an integral you can't compute" move is Monte Carlo, echoing the uncertainty themes in The Black Swan and Thinking in Bets — reason about expectations you can only sample, never fully see.

"These algorithms… make weight adjustments in a direction that lies along the gradient of expected reinforcement."
— Ronald Williams, introducing REINFORCE, 1992

Actor-Critic: A Doer and a Judge

Combining policy gradients with a learned value baseline

The actor proposes; the critic evaluates; the critic's TD error tells the actor which way to move.
◆ Key idea

Actor-critic fuses the two families. The actor \(\pi_\theta\) is a policy trained by policy gradients. The critic \(V_\phi\) is a value function trained by TD. The critic supplies the baseline, so the actor's update uses the low-variance advantage instead of the raw return:

Actor-critic policy gradient
\[ \nabla_\theta J \approx \mathbb{E}\big[\,\nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(a\mid s)\,A(s,a)\,\big], \qquad A(s,a)\approx \delta_t = r + \gamma V_\phi(s') - V_\phi(s) \]

The one-step TD error \(\delta_t\) is itself an unbiased estimate of the advantage — elegant. But one step is biased toward the critic's current guess, while the full Monte Carlo return is unbiased but noisy. Generalized Advantage Estimation (GAE) interpolates between them with a knob \(\lambda\):

GAE — an exponentially-weighted sum of TD errors
\[ \hat{A}_t^{\text{GAE}(\gamma,\lambda)} = \sum_{l=0}^{\infty} (\gamma\lambda)^{l}\,\delta_{t+l} \]

\(\lambda=0\) gives pure one-step TD (low variance, some bias); \(\lambda=1\) gives Monte Carlo (unbiased, high variance). \(\lambda\approx0.95\) is the sweet spot used in PPO.

Intuition

Actor = a doer who tries moves. Critic = a coach who, instead of grading the final score, tells you after each play "that was better/worse than I expected." The coach's running commentary (the advantage) is a far steadier teaching signal than waiting for the final whistle. \(\lambda\) tunes how much you trust the coach's guess vs. how the game actually ends.

😅 Funny take

Actor-critic is every creative duo ever: the actor throws out wild ideas, the critic sighs and says "that one's slightly above average, do more of that." Neither works alone — the actor with no critic is chaos, the critic with no actor is a film-Twitter account.

🤖 AI connection

A2C/A3C popularized this for deep RL. In RLHF, the LLM is the actor and a value head on the same transformer is the critic; GAE with \(\lambda\approx0.95\) computes the advantages that PPO then optimizes. When you hear "PPO has a value model," this is it.

"The critic learns about and critiques whatever policy is currently being followed by the actor."
— Sutton & Barto

Don't Step Too Far: TRPO & PPO

The clipped objective that made policy gradients reliable

PPO flattens the objective outside [1−ε, 1+ε], removing the incentive to take a destructive leap.
◆ Key idea

Vanilla policy gradients are fragile: one large step can collapse the policy, and there's no recovery because the next batch of data comes from the broken policy. TRPO (2015) fixed this by constraining each update to a trust region — keep the new policy within a small KL-divergence of the old one — but it needs second-order optimization and is a pain to implement.

PPO (Schulman et al., 2017) gets ~the same benefit with a first-order clipped surrogate objective. Let \(r_t(\theta)=\dfrac{\pi_\theta(a_t\mid s_t)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(a_t\mid s_t)}\) be the probability ratio. Then maximize:

PPO clipped objective
\[ L^{\text{CLIP}}(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_t\Big[\min\big(r_t(\theta)\,\hat{A}_t,\; \text{clip}(r_t(\theta),\,1-\epsilon,\,1+\epsilon)\,\hat{A}_t\big)\Big] \]

If an action was good (\(\hat A_t>0\)), you want more of it — but the clip caps the reward for pushing \(r_t\) past \(1+\epsilon\) (typically \(\epsilon=0.2\)). If it was bad, the clip caps how far down you shove it. The \(\min\) makes the bound pessimistic, so the update never over-commits on a single noisy batch.

Intuition

PPO is a strict personal trainer: "improve, but no more than 20% heavier than last week." You still progress every session, but you can't ego-lift into a torn hamstring you can't walk back. Small, trust-worthy steps compound; one reckless max-out ends the program. That conservatism is exactly why PPO is stable enough to train giant models.

😅 Funny take

The clip is the friend who physically holds you back at 1am: "I know the gradient says text your ex, but we're capping today's policy update at ±0.2." You wake up grateful your parameters didn't diverge.

🤖 AI connection

PPO was the default RLHF optimizer — InstructGPT and the original ChatGPT were trained with it. The full objective adds a per-token KL penalty to the frozen reference (SFT) model: \(R = r_\phi(x,y) - \beta\,\mathrm{KL}[\pi_\theta \,\|\, \pi_{\text{ref}}]\). That KL term stops the model from drifting into gibberish that games the reward model — the practical face of reward hacking.

🔗 Cross-guide connection

"Small reversible steps beat one big irreversible bet" is the antifragility of Antifragile and the incrementalism of Atomic Habits — bounded updates, compounding gains.

"PPO methods have the stability and reliability of trust-region methods but are much simpler to implement… and empirically perform at least as well."
— Schulman et al., Proximal Policy Optimization, 2017

Aligning LLMs: The RLHF Pipeline & Its Successors

Reward models, PPO, and the DPO / GRPO simplifications

The classic three-stage RLHF pipeline — and the DPO shortcut that folds stages 2 and 3 together.
◆ Key idea

Human values are hard to write as a reward function, so learn the reward from human comparisons. Classic RLHF (InstructGPT, 2022) runs three stages:

  • SFT: fine-tune a base LLM on high-quality demonstrations.
  • Reward model \(r_\phi\): humans rank pairs of responses; train \(r_\phi\) so preferred answers score higher, via the Bradley–Terry loss \(-\log\sigma\big(r_\phi(x,y_w)-r_\phi(x,y_l)\big)\).
  • RL optimization: use PPO (Chapter 11) to make \(\pi_\theta\) maximize \(r_\phi\), minus a KL leash to the SFT model so it stays fluent.

DPO (Direct Preference Optimization, 2023) proved you can skip the separate reward model and the RL loop. A bit of algebra shows the optimal RLHF policy is a closed-form function of the preferences, so you can train directly on the ranked pairs with a simple classification-style loss:

DPO loss (no reward model, no sampling loop)
\[ \mathcal{L}_{\text{DPO}} = -\mathbb{E}\Big[\log \sigma\Big(\beta \log\tfrac{\pi_\theta(y_w\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_w\mid x)} - \beta \log\tfrac{\pi_\theta(y_l\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_l\mid x)}\Big)\Big] \]

GRPO (Group Relative Policy Optimization), from DeepSeekMath/DeepSeek-R1, keeps the PPO-style objective but drops the value network. Instead it samples a group of \(G\) completions per prompt and uses their normalized reward as the advantage: \(\hat A_i = (r_i - \text{mean}(r))/\text{std}(r)\). The group average is the baseline — cheaper, and a natural fit for verifiable-reward reasoning tasks (math, code).

Intuition

PPO: hire a judge (reward model), then coach the student against the judge with a whole value-estimation apparatus. DPO: realize the judge's verdicts already imply the ideal student, and just teach the student to prefer the winners — one clean loss, no RL loop. GRPO: skip the judge's absolute scores; for each question, sample several answers and reward the ones that beat the group average. "Grade on a curve within each question."

⚠ Pitfall — reward hacking & over-optimization

Push too hard on a learned reward and the policy finds adversarial outputs that fool \(r_\phi\) without being genuinely good (sycophancy, verbosity, formatting tricks). The KL penalty and early stopping are the main defenses; it's Goodhart's Law — "when a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure" — in neural form.

😅 Funny take

RLHF is teaching an alien to be polite by having it write a million dinner-party toasts while a tired human clicks "better"/"worse." DPO is realizing you can skip building the alien-etiquette robot judge and just show it your thumbs-up pile. GRPO is grading the alien's ten attempts against each other and keeping the least-alarming three.

🤖 AI connection

This is the frontier as of 2024–25: DPO and its variants (IPO, KTO) dominate open-source alignment for their simplicity; GRPO powers reasoning models trained with verifiable rewards. All of it descends from the humble policy-gradient rule on the slide — the reward source and the variance-reduction trick change, but \(\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta\) weighted by "how good was this?" is always the beating heart.

Story Spotlight · The Machines That Taught Themselves

TD-Gammon (1992) → AlphaGo (2016) → AlphaZero (2017)

In 1992 Gerald Tesauro's TD-Gammon learned backgammon almost from scratch using TD(λ) and a neural network, playing hundreds of thousands of games against itself. It reached near-world-champion strength and, startlingly, invented opening strategies that human experts had dismissed for centuries — then adopted after the machine vindicated them. It was the first loud proof that TD learning + neural nets + self-play could produce genuinely creative expertise.

Two decades later AlphaGo (DeepMind, 2016) beat Lee Sedol 4–1 at Go, a game with more board positions than atoms in the universe — far beyond any Bellman sweep. It combined a policy network, a value network, and Monte Carlo Tree Search, bootstrapped from human games then refined by RL self-play. Move 37 of game 2 was so alien and so beautiful that commentators assumed it was a bug; it was strategy no human had considered.

AlphaZero (2017) then dropped the human data entirely: starting from random play and knowing only the rules, pure self-play RL surpassed every prior program in Go, chess, and shogi within hours. The lesson that echoes into RLHF: given a reward signal and the freedom to explore, an RL agent can climb past the ceiling of its human teachers.

Story Spotlight · How RL Made LLMs Helpful

From a fluent-but-feral base model to InstructGPT and ChatGPT (2022)

A raw pretrained language model is a magnificent autocomplete and a hopeless assistant — ask it a question and it might reply with more questions, because that's what the internet does. OpenAI's InstructGPT (2022) showed that RLHF could fix this: humans wrote demonstrations (SFT), ranked model outputs to train a reward model, then used PPO to optimize the policy against that reward with a KL leash to stay coherent.

The result stunned even its authors: a 1.3B-parameter InstructGPT model was preferred by human raters over the 175B GPT-3 — a 100× smaller model made more useful purely by aligning it with human preference. That recipe, wrapped in a chat interface, became ChatGPT. The policy-gradient rule on your slide is the mathematical seed of that entire phenomenon.

The plot twist of 2023–2025: much of the heavy RL machinery turned out to be optional. DPO hit similar quality by training directly on preferences with no reward model and no sampling loop, and GRPO stripped out the value network to train the reasoning models (like DeepSeek-R1) that learn to think step-by-step from verifiable rewards. The field keeps asking: how little RL can we get away with?

The RL Cheatsheet

Every core formula in one place — bookmark this

Return

\(G_t=\sum_{k\ge0}\gamma^k r_{t+k}\)

Discounted sum of future rewards. The thing we maximize.

Value functions

\(V^\pi(s)=\mathbb{E}[G_t|s]\), \(\;Q^\pi(s,a)=\mathbb{E}[G_t|s,a]\)

Expected return from a state / state-action pair.

Advantage

\(A(s,a)=Q(s,a)-V(s)\)

How much better than average. The signal PPO/GRPO reinforce.

Bellman optimality

\(Q^*(s,a)=R+\gamma\sum_{s'}P\,\max_{a'}Q^*(s',a')\)

Fixed point defining optimal behavior.

Value iteration

\(V_{k+1}(s)\leftarrow\max_a[R+\gamma\sum_{s'}P\,V_k(s')]\)

Model-based; a γ-contraction → V*.

TD(0) error

\(\delta_t=r+\gamma V(s')-V(s)\)

Prediction error; bootstrapped online learning signal.

Q-learning

\(Q\leftarrow Q+\alpha[r+\gamma\max_{a'}Q(s',a')-Q]\)

Off-policy control. DQN = this + deep net + replay + target net.

Policy gradient (REINFORCE)

\(\nabla_\theta J=\mathbb{E}[R\,\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta]\)

The slide. Sample completions, weight log-prob grads by reward.

Actor-critic / GAE

\(\nabla_\theta J\approx\mathbb{E}[\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta\,\hat A_t]\), \(\;\hat A_t=\sum_l(\gamma\lambda)^l\delta_{t+l}\)

Advantage baseline cuts variance.

PPO clip

\(L=\mathbb{E}[\min(r_t\hat A_t,\text{clip}(r_t,1\pm\epsilon)\hat A_t)]\)

Bounded steps → stable. RLHF default.

DPO

\(-\log\sigma(\beta\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y_w)}{\pi_{ref}(y_w)}-\beta\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y_l)}{\pi_{ref}(y_l)})\)

No reward model, no RL loop. Train on preference pairs.

GRPO advantage

\(\hat A_i=(r_i-\text{mean}(r))/\text{std}(r)\)

Group-relative baseline; drops the value network.
