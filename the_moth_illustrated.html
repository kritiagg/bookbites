How to Tell a Story

The Art of Vulnerability & Connection

1

Have Real Stakes

What Makes Stakes Real?

  • You could win something meaningful (love, career, redemption)
  • You could lose something precious (dignity, trust, opportunity)
  • The outcome was genuinely uncertain when it happened
  • Your audience recognizes why it mattered to you

The Funny Take

"My story about losing a sock in the laundry has low stakes. My story about losing custody of my kid? Everyone leans forward. Guess which one people remember."

🤖 AI Connection

Like storytelling, good prompts signal what's at stake. A vague prompt has low stakes (any answer is fine). A specific, detailed prompt raises the stakes—you need precision because precision matters.

📚 Cross-Book Connection

Influence: Stories with high stakes trigger emotional investment, which Cialdini shows is essential for persuasion.

"Vulnerability builds trust. But raw vulnerability without stakes is just complaining."

— The Moth Community

2

Be Vulnerable

Why Vulnerability Works

  • Audiences connect with flaws, not achievements
  • Your embarrassment becomes their permission to feel
  • Perfection is boring; mess is magnetic
  • The thing you're most afraid to say is often the thing they need to hear

The Funny Take

"You want a standing ovation? Tell them you cried. Tell them you were wrong. Tell them you bought a $400 face cream and it made you break out. Perfection? Nobody claps for perfect."

🤖 AI Connection

AI trained to sound "perfect" can feel sterile. The most useful AI responses admit uncertainty: "I don't know," "I might be wrong," "Here's what I'm confident about." That's AI vulnerability.

📚 Cross-Book Connection

Brené Brown (Daring Greatly): Vulnerability isn't weakness. It's the most honest, courageous way to connect.

"People don't connect with your strength. They connect with your mess."

— The Moth

3

Start Strong

The 10-Second Rule

  • You have 10 seconds to make them care
  • Start with a question, shock, or vivid detail—not context
  • Don't begin with "So, let me tell you a story about..."
  • Jump into a moment: action, dialogue, sensory overload

The Funny Take

"If your first sentence is 'I grew up in Ohio,' you've already lost them. If it's 'I woke up in my car at 4 AM with a llama,' they're yours forever."

🤖 AI Connection

A strong opening in a prompt is like a strong story opening. "Summarize this" gets a generic response. "Summarize this in the voice of a frustrated engineer" immediately signals what matters.

📚 Cross-Book Connection

Thinking, Fast and Slow: Your audience's System 1 (automatic thinking) decides in seconds. Hook that system first.

"The first sentence is a door. Make it impossible to close."

— The Moth

4

Show Transformation

The Core of Every Story

  • Who were you *before* this thing happened?
  • What belief did you hold? What were you blind to?
  • Who are you *now* after the experience?
  • The transformation is the story, not the events

The Funny Take

"'I went to Paris' is a vacation. 'I went to Paris thinking I'd find myself and instead found my ex' is a story. Change is your plot device."

🤖 AI Connection

The best AI outputs show how someone's thinking changed. Not "here's information" but "here's how this shifts what we know."

📚 Cross-Book Connection

Flow: Growth happens at the edge of your skill. A transformation story shows you crossed that edge.

"A story is not a change of circumstance. It's a change of self."

— The Moth

5

Sensory Details

Show, Don't Tell

  • Not "I was scared" → "My hands were shaking"
  • Not "It was beautiful" → "The light hit her face like honey"
  • Not "He was angry" → "His jaw clenched. His breathing stopped."
  • Sensory details make emotions *real* instead of reported

The Funny Take

"Saying 'I was nervous' is lazy. Describing how you could taste your own heartbeat? Now we're nervous too. That's the goal."

🤖 AI Connection

Generic output: "The weather was bad." Specific: "Rain hammered the windows. The air tasted like copper." Details make AI feel alive.

📚 Cross-Book Connection

Thinking, Fast and Slow: Vivid details trigger System 1 emotion. Abstract ideas trigger System 2 (slow) thinking.

"People don't remember what you felt. They remember what they felt when you described it."

— The Moth

6

Know Your Audience

Room-Specific Stories

  • Same story, different emphasis for different rooms
  • Tech crowd wants the problem-solving arc
  • Artists want the emotional/creative journey
  • Strangers need more context; friends need less

The Funny Take

"Your story about leaving your startup is one thing at a hackathon and another thing at a therapist's office. Same person, different rooms, different truths."

🤖 AI Connection

Audience awareness is prompt engineering. A prompt for "explain like I'm 5" gets a different (better) answer than "explain to a PhD."

📚 Cross-Book Connection

Influence: Persuasion depends on audience values. Different audiences, different appeals.

"The best story changes itself based on who's listening."

— The Moth

7

Edit Ruthlessly

Ruthless Editing Principles

  • Every sentence must earn its place
  • Kill the backstory no one asked for
  • Delete explanations—show, don't explain
  • If you love a line and it doesn't serve the story, delete it anyway

The Funny Take

"Your 3-minute story doesn't need the full history of your childhood trauma. It needs the one sentence that makes it matter. Cut everything else."

🤖 AI Connection

Prompt engineering is about deleting noise. "Write about AI" gets 10 pages. "Write 100 words about AI bias in hiring" gets precision.

📚 Cross-Book Connection

Atomic Habits: Small, focused actions > long, unfocused effort. Same with stories.

"Editing isn't about cutting. It's about finding the story hidden inside your draft."

— The Moth

Story Spotlights

The Babysitter's Chaos

A teenager is left alone with three younger siblings for the first time. Everything goes wrong. Milk spills. Someone's bleeding (a nosebleed). The youngest won't stop crying. But somewhere in the chaos, the babysitter figures it out—not by being perfect, but by admitting she's terrified and asking for help. The siblings don't remember the chaos. They remember her crying laughing with them at midnight.

The Wrong Funeral

A woman accidentally walks into a stranger's funeral at the cemetery. She sits through the whole thing before realizing her mistake. Instead of leaving, she stays. As she listens to people speak about this stranger's life—a teacher, a friend, someone who mattered—something cracks open in her about her own grief, her own losses, the people she never said goodbye to. She ends up talking to the family afterward. They never knew her, but they needed someone to show up that day.

The Moth Cheatsheet

1. Real Stakes

What could you win or lose? If the answer is "nothing," you don't have a story yet.

2. Vulnerability

Show your mess. Your flaws are the bridge to their hearts.

3. Strong Start

10 seconds. Hook them with action, dialogue, or a vivid detail.

4. Transformation

Before and after. Who were you? Who are you now? That's your plot.

5. Sensory Details

Not "I was scared"—"My hands shook." Make them *feel* it.

6. Know Your Room

Same story, different audience, different emphasis. Adapt.

7. Edit Ruthlessly

Every sentence earns its place. Kill your darlings. Find the core story hiding inside.
