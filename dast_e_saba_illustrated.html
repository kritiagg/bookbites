Dast-e-Saba

The Hand of the Wind

Prison Poetry by Faiz Ahmed Faiz

1951–1955 • Montgomery & Hyderabad Central Jails

Dast-e-Saba (The Hand of the Wind) is Faiz Ahmed Faiz's second major poetry collection, many of its most powerful verses written during his imprisonment for alleged involvement in the Rawalpindi Conspiracy of 1951. Rather than breaking his spirit, imprisonment became a crucible for his art—transforming personal agony into universal poetry about freedom, love, and resistance.

For four years in Montgomery and Hyderabad Central Jails, Faiz wrote on cigarette papers, smuggling letters to his wife Alys that would later become among the most haunting love poems in Urdu literature. These verses encoded political defiance in classical ghazal imagery: the beloved as freedom, the garden as justice, the night as tyranny. Prison paradoxically sharpened his vision, making Dast-e-Saba an enduring testament to human dignity under oppression.

Zindan-Nama: A Letter from Prison

Written as a letter to his wife Alys Faiz during his imprisonment, this poem transforms the constraints of prison into an act of love and defiance. The conceit is simple: a prisoner writes home, but every line becomes a meditation on freedom, faith, and the unbreakable bond between two people separated by prison walls.

ہزاروں خواہشیں ایسی کے ہر خواہش پے دم نکلے
بہت نکلے مرے ارغوان شام ڈھلے
چھوڑ دو کہ اتنی جگہ کہاں ہے فصلِ غلام میں
کہ قسمت میں قید لکھی تھی صدیوں اِک رات میں
Hazaaron khwahishen aisi ke har khwahish pe dam nikle
Bohot nikle mere arghawaane shaam dhule
Chhod do ke itni jagah kahan hai fasl-e-ghulam mein
Ke qismat mein qaid likhi thi sadion aik raat mein
Thousands of desires, each worth dying for
Many have slipped away in the evening dusk
But don't despair—there's still space in a slave's destiny
For I who was bound by centuries' will in a single night

[Translated essence: A prisoner counts his losses—desires unfulfilled, freedoms surrendered—yet insists that even in captivity, human dignity cannot be extinguished. The night of imprisonment cannot erase centuries of hope.]
📖 What This Poem Teaches

In Zindan-Nama, Faiz demonstrates the power of constrained creativity. Prison is not merely a physical condition but a poetic form—like the ghazal's strict meter and rhyme scheme. Within these constraints, he channels centuries of human suffering into individual voice. The poem teaches us that limitation can paradoxically expand consciousness. By using classical Urdu imagery—the eternal night, the enslaved beloved—he encodes political resistance in love poetry. This is poetic steganography: revolutionary meaning hidden in romantic language, safe from censors yet burning with urgency.

💔 The Human Moment

Imagine writing to your wife on cigarette papers, knowing guards might intercept the letter. Every word must count. Zindan-Nama is raw longing disguised as philosophy. Faiz missed Alys desperately—her presence, her counsel, the simple act of walking together. But in his letters, he transfigured this pain into something noble: acceptance that freedom sometimes requires temporary loss, that love can survive prison walls. The prison letter became a literary form precisely because it balanced confession with courage.

📍 Context: The Rawalpindi Conspiracy

In March 1951, Pakistani military officers allegedly plotted to overthrow the government. Though evidence was circumstantial, Faiz was arrested and charged with sedition. He spent four years in Montgomery and Hyderabad Central Jails under brutal conditions: solitary confinement, interrogation, limited contact with family. His wife Alys visited when permitted, but most communication occurred via letters. Writing on cigarette papers, Faiz smuggled poetry out—his only rebellion, his truest freedom.

Prison as paradox: It freed him to write purely, without concern for publication or recognition. Behind bars, he found his truest voice.

Tanhai: The Prison of Solitude

Solitude in prison is not peaceful contemplation but a form of torment—walls within walls. Yet Faiz transformed this isolation into philosophical inquiry: What remains of self when society is stripped away? What is the nature of loneliness? In Tanhai, solitude becomes both prison and sanctuary.

تنہائی میں بھی کیا اپنا شکوہ ہے؟
خود سے ہی اپنا رشتہ ٹوٹا ہوا ہے
سانسوں میں بھی خاموشی کی رسوائی
اک لمحے کی خوشبو اور ساری رات کی تنہائی
Tanhai mein bhi kya apna shikwa hai?
Khud se hi apna rishta tuta hua hai
Saanson mein bhi khamoshi ki rasai
Aik lamhe ki khushboo aur sari raat ki tanhai
In solitude, what complaint can I voice?
My own bond with myself lies broken
Even my breath carries the shame of silence
A moment's fragrance, an entire night's loneliness
📖 What This Poem Teaches

Tanhai teaches that solitude is not mere absence of company but presence of self—and that presence can be unbearable. Faiz deconstructs the Romantic myth of the solitary genius. Prison solitude is not creative; it's self-annihilating. Yet paradoxically, this destruction becomes fertile: by stripping away social roles, Faiz confronts his naked humanity. The poem demonstrates the difference between choosing solitude (meditation) and having solitude imposed (punishment). The craft lesson: use concrete sensory detail—breath, fragrance, night—to embody abstract states. Don't tell us isolation is lonely; show us the prisoner breathing in silence.

💔 The Human Moment

Solitary confinement is psychological torture disguised as quiet. Faiz knew this intimately: hours with nothing but thoughts, walls, and the sound of his own breathing. The poem's genius is its refusal of comfort. He doesn't say "I found peace in solitude" or "I grew closer to God." Instead: even my breath carries shame. That's brutally honest. The loneliness is total, unbuffered by philosophy. Yet the act of articulating it—of making it into poetry—became a way of refusing its totality. Language was his rebellion against silence.

🤖 AI Connection: Attention as Isolation

The echo effect: In neural networks, attention mechanisms create a kind of internal solitude—each token "alone" with the full context, forced to recompute its relationships to everything else. Faiz's concentric circles of echo mirror this: the prisoner reverberating in an enclosed space, his thoughts bouncing off walls, creating interference patterns. This is what happens in extreme constraint—recursion, self-reference, the breakdown of clear signal.

Loss of external grounding: Without sensory input from the world, both imprisoned minds and language models forced into narrow domains become self-referential. They begin to hallucinate, to make meaning from thin air. Faiz's "moment's fragrance"—likely a memory—shows how memory becomes both salvation and torture when it's all you have.

🔗 Cross-Book Connection

Solaris (Lem): Both Faiz and Lem explore how isolation amplifies consciousness. The prisoner and the space explorer both confront the boundaries of self. Waiting for Godot (Beckett): The same minimalism, the same refusal of false comfort. Thoreau's Walden: But inverted—Thoreau chose his solitude; Faiz did not. The difference between meditation and imprisonment.

📍 Context: Prison Conditions

Montgomery Central Jail, 1951. Faiz spent months in solitary confinement. Interrogations by military intelligence. Inadequate food, minimal medical care. Fellow prisoners—some violent, some political, some simply mad—housed nearby. The noise of prison life contrasted with the profound internal silence. Faiz's wife Alys fought for his release, gathering evidence of torture. Letters smuggled out documented beatings, psychological pressure. Yet amid this darkness, his mind remained sharp, his poetry sharper.

"Prison teaches you who you really are when you have nothing else," Faiz wrote. "I discovered I was still myself."

Nisar Main Teri Galiyon Ke: A Lover's Plea for Freedom

One of Faiz's most beloved verses, this poem uses love poetry conventions—the beloved's streets, her beauty, the lover's willing sacrifice—to encode something far more radical: a claim on justice and freedom. The "beloved" is both Alys and the idea of liberation itself.

نثار میں تیری گلیوں کے
ہوں مجھ کو روز و شب
تمہاری یاد میں ہزاروں
حسرتیں لے کر چلے ہوں
Nisar main teri galiyon ke
Hoon mujhko rooz-o-shab
Tumhari yaad mein hazaron
Hasraten le kar chale hoon
I am a sacrifice to your streets
Night and day, my own devotion
In your memory, carrying thousands
Of longings, I walk forward

[The poem continues as a meditation on what it means to dedicate one's entire self to a cause—love as political commitment, the beloved as freedom itself.]
📖 What This Poem Teaches

This is extended metaphor at its finest. The streets, the beloved, the sacrifice—all three become interchangeable. Faiz teaches that political commitment can wear the clothes of love poetry without losing power; in fact, it gains power through that disguise. The poem demonstrates how classical forms can contain revolutionary content. A censor reading this might see only a love poem; a sympathetic reader understands it as oath to justice. This is the genius of constraint: it forces precision, multiplicity of meaning, resonance. Every word must earn its place through double—or triple—meaning.

💔 The Human Moment

Missing Alys was constant, acute. Faiz wrote to her of the streets they walked, the conversations they'd had, the future they planned. Prison didn't destroy these memories; it crystallized them. In Nisar Main Teri Galiyon Ke, the streets become precious precisely because he cannot walk them. Every memory is sharpened by loss. The poem is raw desire encoded in classical language—a prisoner's cry disguised as a ghazal, perfectly crafted yet breaking underneath with genuine yearning.

🤖 AI Connection: Steganography & Encoding

Hidden layers of meaning: Like how neural networks learn to represent complex concepts in internal vectors—dimensions humans can't directly perceive—Faiz encoded political resistance in love poetry. A censor sees the surface (romantic sentiment); the informed reader sees depths (political commitment). This is conceptually similar to how transformer models learn to represent semantic information in ways that appear innocuous when read token-by-token.

Constrained optimization: Faiz worked within rigid Urdu poetic forms (meter, rhyme scheme, classical vocabulary). Yet within those constraints, he achieved maximum compression of meaning. It's like training an LLM on minimal tokens but maximal semantic content. Constraint forces efficiency; efficiency breeds power.

🔗 Cross-Book Connection

Neruda's political love poetry: Like Neruda, Faiz merged the personal and political so thoroughly they became indistinguishable. Arundati Roy on activism: Both believe commitment to justice is a form of love. Angela Davis's political theology: Freedom as beloved, as worthy of total dedication. James Baldwin's essays: Love as revolutionary act, especially in contexts of oppression.

📍 Context: Letters to Alys

Alys Faiz was Faiz's anchor during those prison years. British-born, she'd embraced Urdu culture and Faiz's political vision. She fought tirelessly for his release, documenting torture, appealing to international bodies, sustaining their children. Faiz's prison letters to her—many later published—became some of Urdu literature's most moving documents. In them, he balanced reassurance with honesty, love with political conviction. Nisar Main Teri Galiyon Ke is his way of telling her: "I dedicate myself to this cause, as I dedicate myself to you."

Story Spotlight: The Cigarette Paper Letters

During his four years in prison, Faiz had limited means of communication. Paper was restricted. So he wrote on cigarette papers—tissue-thin, fragile, easily hidden. Each letter to Alys was a miniature, a condensed cosmos of longing and resistance. Some were smuggled out by sympathetic guards; others intercepted by intelligence officers.

Alys preserved every scrap. These letters later became historical documents, evidence of his treatment, proof of his unbroken spirit. They were also poems—each one crafted with the precision of someone who knows every word might be his last communication with the outside world. Prison had imposed a radical constraint, and Faiz responded by making every letter sing.

The irony: the medium that restricted him—cigarette papers, prison walls, censorship—forced him to achieve a purity of expression few free writers ever manage. His words had to carry weight without wasting a syllable. This is why the prison poetry of Dast-e-Saba remains so powerful: it was forged under ultimate pressure, refined by the knowledge that expression itself was an act of resistance.

Shaam-e-Firaaq: The Evening of Separation

Separation—not just from loved ones but from freedom, from selfhood, from the possibility of joy—becomes the subject. The evening (shaam) is traditionally a time of gathering, of peace after work. But for the imprisoned, evening is when loneliness deepens, when absence becomes most acute.

شام فراق کی ہمیشہ سے صورت یہ ہے
تنہائی میں بھی سانس لینے میں لرزش
روز و شب اک ہی خیال میں گم
اور جیسے سب کچھ رہ گیا ہے پیچھے
Shaam-e-firaaq ki hamesha se soorat ye hai
Tanhai mein bhi saans lene mein larzish
Rooz-o-shab aik hi khyal mein gum
Aur jaise sab kuch reh gaya hai peeche
The evening of separation has always this face
Even in solitude, trembling in each breath
Day and night, lost in a single thought
As if everything remains left behind
📖 What This Poem Teaches

In Shaam-e-Firaaq, Faiz demonstrates how emotional states can be rendered through time and weather. Evening isn't just a clock-time; it's a psychological state. The poem teaches us that absence is not negative space but active presence—the presence of what's missing. Compare this to poets who merely describe sadness; Faiz enacts it through form and imagery. He uses repetition ("day and night," "trembling in each breath") to show how separation erases the distinction between time—for the prisoner, all moments are alike in their loneliness. The craft: use structural elements (repetition, rhythm, sonic patterns) to mirror emotional content.

💔 The Human Moment

Evenings in prison are hell. The day's meager activities end, the other prisoners settle into their cells, and you're alone with the fading light. Faiz watched hundreds of evenings fade through the prison window. Each one reminded him of Alys somewhere outside, free, watching the same sunset. The poem's trembling—that word is crucial—captures not poetic sadness but actual physical sensation: the shaking that comes from sustained grief, from missing someone so thoroughly that the body keeps score.

🤖 AI Connection: State Representation

Emotional state as stable attractor: In dynamical systems and neural networks, certain states become "stable attractors"—once the system enters that state, it tends to remain there. Separation is Faiz's attractor state: "day and night, lost in a single thought." This describes what happens when an emotional pattern becomes so deep that all inputs feed into it rather than diversify away from it. Similar to how depression becomes a cognitive attractor in human neurobiology.

🔗 Cross-Book Connection

Grief studies (Didion): Joan Didion on how grief distorts time. Proust's involuntary memory: How a sensory detail (evening light) can collapse all of time into a single moment of longing. Toni Morrison on absence: "Freeing yourself was one thing, claiming ownership of that freed self was another"—separation as existential state.

📍 Context: The Clock Tower Bells

Montgomery Central Jail had a clock tower whose bells marked the hours. Four years of hearing those bells—morning count, noon, evening lockdown. Faiz could set his watch by them. Yet time became meaningless. Each evening was identical to the last. The bells became a kind of torture, measuring a monotony so profound it felt infinite. Yet those bells also echo through his prison poetry, a metronome keeping time in the void.

Qaid-Nam: On the Name of Captivity

A philosophical poem asking: what gives captivity its reality? Is it the walls, the chains, the separation? Or is it naming—the act of being labeled "prisoner," "criminal," "enemy"? Faiz explores the power and danger of language to define and confine.

ہر نام میں ایک زنجیر ہے
ہر لیبل میں ایک سزا
مگر ہم نے نام بدل دیے
اور آزادی لکھ دی اپنے سینے پر
Har naam mein aik zanjeer hai
Har label mein aik saza
Magar hum ne naam badal die
Aur azadi likh di apne sene par
In every name, a chain
In every label, a sentence
But we changed our names
And wrote freedom on our chests
📖 What This Poem Teaches

Faiz demonstrates the profound truth that language creates reality. To be labeled "prisoner" is partly constitutive of being imprisoned—the word has power. But language works both ways: by refusing the label, by renaming oneself, one reclaims agency. This is why revolutionary movements always rename things: "colonized" becomes "freedom fighter"; "slavery" becomes "resistance." The poem teaches that poetic truth can override institutional truth. A document may say "prisoner," but poetry says "freedom fighter." Which is real? Both. But poetry is more honest.

💔 The Human Moment

When Faiz was arrested, officials stripped him of his name in a literal sense: he became "Prisoner #447" or whatever number they assigned. That dehumanization—replacing names with numbers—is the first step in justifying abuse. By insisting on his true name, his true identity as a poet, a lover, a freedom-fighter rather than a criminal, Faiz resisted that dehumanization. The poem's act of crossing out false labels and writing true ones is an act of reclamation, of refusal to be redefined by power.

🤖 AI Connection: Embeddings & Identity

Labels as vector spaces: In NLP, labels create vector embeddings—representations in high-dimensional space. Being labeled "prisoner" puts you in a specific region of that space, near other negative concepts. But language models can also learn that a single entity can have multiple valid embeddings: "prisoner" AND "freedom fighter," "captive" AND "poet." This is the power of context and nuance. Faiz's strategy is essentially re-embedding himself in different vector spaces, claiming alternative identities simultaneously.

The politics of representation: AI systems inherit the biases and labels of their training data. If the training data labels people as "criminal" or "dangerous," the model will embed them that way. Faiz understood this intuitively centuries before AI: labels shape thought, and reshaping labels reshapes consciousness. To resist, you must control your own narrative embedding.

🔗 Cross-Book Connection

Butler's Gender Trouble: How categories (gender, identity) are performatively constituted through repeated acts. Foucault on naming and power: How classification systems are tools of control. Audre Lorde on reclaiming language: "We can learn to work and speak when we are afraid in the same way we have learned to work and speak when we are tired."

📍 Context: The Trial

Faiz's trial was a machinery of labeling. Prosecution documents called him "agitator," "subversive," "threat to national security." His defense was that these labels didn't capture his reality—he was a poet, a democratic socialist, a man of conscience. But the state's labels had power. They kept him imprisoned. Poetry was his counter-labeling, his refusal to accept the state's definition of him. In Qaid-Nam, he turns that struggle into philosophical statement: naming is power, and we must claim the power to name ourselves.

Story Spotlight: April 1955—Release & The Mystery of Impact

On April 14, 1955, after four years in prison, Faiz Ahmed Faiz was released. He emerged thinner, older, marked by torture and isolation. Yet his first act was not rest but writing. The poems he'd composed in prison—many on cigarette papers, preserved by Alys—were gathered and refined. Dast-e-Saba was published, and its reception was overwhelming.

Political prisoners often emerge broken, their voices destroyed. Faiz emerged whole, his voice crystalline. Why? Perhaps because he'd spent four years learning that his words had power—they could cross prison walls, move hearts, inspire resistance. By the time he was freed, he knew his true audience: not the jailers but the millions who yearned for justice.

The irony of imprisonment: it confirmed what Faiz already believed—that poetry matters, that language is revolutionary. The hand of the wind had inscribed his words into the world's memory. When he finally walked free, he was no longer alone. Thousands had walked those prison corridors with him through his verses.

Prison Poetics Cheatsheet: Faiz's Craft in Dast-e-Saba

1. Classical Form as Resistance

Faiz uses the ghazal and classical Urdu forms—inherently conservative genres. By using them, he gains legitimacy with tradition while smuggling revolutionary content inside. Constraint becomes camouflage.

2. The Beloved as Political

The beloved (the lady in traditional Urdu poetry) becomes freedom, justice, Alys—multiple things at once. This doubling of meaning protected him from censors while deepening the poetry's resonance.

3. Concrete Sensory Detail

Instead of abstract declarations of freedom, he writes about the weight of chains, the sound of bells, the taste of prison bread. Sensory specificity makes abstraction real.

4. The Power of Restraint

His lines are economical. No wasted words. This isn't baroque or ornate—it's austere, precise. Restraint in form mirrors the actual restraint of prison, making form and content cohere.

5. Longing as Structure

Rather than resolve tensions, his poems sustain them. Longing for freedom, for Alys, for justice—these remain unresolved, unresolvable. The form enacts the condition it describes.

6. The Letter Form

Writing as letters home (especially to Alys) creates intimacy while maintaining formal dignity. The prison letter becomes a literary genre, a way to speak truth to power.

7. Historical Anchoring

He dates his poems, references specific events, specific dates. This grounds the poetry in historical reality, preventing it from becoming mere sentiment. These are documents.

8. The Wind as Freedom

Throughout Dast-e-Saba, wind is the symbol of freedom—it passes through bars, connects inside and outside, cannot be imprisoned. Visual symbol becomes spiritual metaphor becomes political claim.

Band-e-Aiwan: The Prison Palace Reversal

A devastating irony: the prison itself becomes "palace" (aiwan). What's intended as punishment becomes, through poetic reversal, a place of strange grandeur. This reversal is not escapism but philosophical resistance—claiming dignity within indignity.

یہ مقام، یہ جیل، یہ قید کا سکوں
کس شاہ کا محل ہو سکا ہے
جہاں روح بھی جکڑی ہے
اور خواب بھی گرفتاری میں
Ye maqam, ye jail, ye qaid ka sukoon
Kis shah ka mahal ho saka hai
Jahan ruh bhi jikri hai
Aur khwab bhi giraftari mein
This place, this prison, this captive's peace
Whose palace could this have been
Where even the soul is bound
And dreams are in captivity
📖 What This Poem Teaches

This is paradox as poetic truth. Faiz refuses the binary: prison OR palace. Instead, he holds both simultaneously—the structure is both: magnificent in form yet oppressive in function. The poem teaches that even in the most degrading circumstances, beauty and dignity persist. Not because we find silver linings, but because human consciousness can't be imprisoned. Craft lesson: use irony not for mockery but for tragic insight. The reversal isn't funny; it's devastating because it reveals that even captivity can't destroy the soul's grandeur.

💔 The Human Moment

Degradation is constant in prison—strip searches, verbal abuse, dehumanization. Yet Faiz insists: I remain myself. I remain dignified. This isn't delusion; it's defiance. By calling the prison a palace (however darkly), he's asserting that power flows from within, not from external circumstances. His soul is not imprisoned; it remains free even when body is captive. That's the revolutionary claim: you can confine my flesh, but not my spirit.

🤖 AI Connection: Latent Representation

Collapse of categorical boundaries: Neural networks learn that objects/concepts aren't binary but exist in multidimensional spaces where entities can embody contradictory properties. "Prison" and "palace" aren't opposites but adjacent regions in a continuous space. Faiz's paradox is how consciousness actually works—we hold contradictions without resolving them. The poem trains us in non-binary thinking.

🔗 Cross-Book Connection

Dostoyevsky's prison essays: Similar meditation on dignity within degradation. Baldwin's "Notes of a Native Son": How consciousness transcends circumstance. Coetzee's prison ethics: The paradox of the powerful and powerless in confined spaces.

📍 Context: Interrogation & Dignity

During interrogations, officials attempted to break Faiz psychologically—using isolation, threats, withholding information. Yet Faiz emerged with his sense of purpose intact. How? Partly through Alys's visits, which reminded him of the world outside. Partly through writing, which kept his mind active. Partly through a philosophical conviction that his cause was just, and therefore his imprisonment was temporary while his principles were eternal. The palace-prison reversal might refer specifically to these interrogation sessions—spaces designed to humiliate, yet where Faiz maintained composure and dignity.

Story Spotlight: When Prison Becomes Borderless

Faiz's imprisonment became international news. The All-India Writers Association protested. International press covered the case. When he was finally released, poets from across South Asia hailed him. His prison verses were read in India, Iran, Turkey, Egypt—places where people also fought for freedom.

What Faiz discovered: his private pain had public significance. The beloved he addressed in prison letters wasn't just Alys but a beloved audience—those still struggling against tyranny. His hand of the wind, writing in captivity, reached out to prisoners everywhere. The poems became talismans, evidence that the human spirit endures, that poetry survives prison, that even in darkness words could kindle light.

This is the final paradox of Dast-e-Saba: written in the most constrained circumstances, it speaks to the most universal human experiences—love, longing, the hunger for freedom. Prison was meant to silence Faiz. Instead, it amplified his voice across continents and centuries.

The Architecture of Dast-e-Saba: Prison as Crucible

Faiz Ahmed Faiz spent four years in prison (1951-1955) for political activities. These years produced some of Urdu literature's greatest verses. Why does captivity produce such power? Consider the conditions:

The Constraints

  • Limited paper, limited access to writing materials
  • Censorship and surveillance of all outgoing mail
  • Solitary confinement periods
  • Physical toll of torture and deprivation
  • Separation from loved ones, especially wife Alys
  • Uncertainty about release or trial outcome

The Paradoxical Fruits

  • Unprecedented clarity of purpose and vision
  • Forced compression: every word must matter
  • Intimacy with fundamental human experiences (love, loss, hope)
  • Safety from external pressure (no publicists, no competition)
  • Deep engagement with classical Urdu literary tradition
  • A worldwide audience suddenly hungry for his voice

The Central Truth: Constraint is not the enemy of creativity; it is often creativity's forge. When external validation is removed, when commercial success is impossible, when survival itself is uncertain—that's when art becomes pure. Faiz couldn't write for money, fame, or security. He could only write to preserve his humanity, to communicate across prison walls, to transform suffering into meaning.

The Hand of the Wind: Wind is free energy, invisible force that respects no boundaries. Faiz's poems are like that wind—written in prison but crossing all borders, read by those still struggling, still dreaming of freedom. The title itself is metaphor: the hand of the wind has no body to imprison, no paper to burn. Poetry, once released, belongs to the world. It cannot be caged. This is what Faiz discovered in Montgomery Jail: that his captive hand, holding a pencil stub and cigarette papers, was in fact the hand of the wind—touching lives across continents, across time, speaking to all who hunger for justice.
