Nudge

Improving Decisions About Health, Wealth, and Happiness

Richard Thaler & Cass Sunstein

1
Choice Architecture: Design the Frame

The way choices are presented dramatically changes which option people select, even when the underlying options are identical. You can't avoid designing a choice architecture—even by doing nothing, you've designed one. Make it intentional, not accidental.

Real Examples:
  • Menu design: placement of options changes what people order by 20-40%
  • Organ donation defaults: opt-in vs opt-out changes participation from 4% to 88%
  • Investment portfolios: same options arranged differently get different allocation of capital
"You can't design a choice architecture that doesn't exist. Even showing nothing at all is a design choice. So you might as well design one that helps people make better decisions."
AI Connection: Prompt design is choice architecture for AI. The way you frame the task determines the outputs. Same capability, different frame = different results.
Cross-Book Connection: Atomic Habits explores how environmental design (choice architecture) changes behavior.
"Any change in the way options are presented can have an important effect on what people choose."
2
Libertarian Paternalism: Freedom + Guidance

The best approach combines freedom with paternalism: preserve people's right to choose whatever they want, but design the default to guide them toward better outcomes. Give people all options while making the best option the easiest one.

Real Examples:
  • Retirement plans: auto-enroll at high savings rate, but let people opt out or reduce
  • Nutritional defaults: healthy food placement at eye level, junk food below
  • Privacy settings: safe defaults, but users can customize if they choose
"It's not nanny-stating if you're still free to choose the bad option. It's just... making it slightly inconvenient. Like a wise parent watching their kid run toward a cliff but not physically stopping them."
AI Connection: Constitutional AI uses libertarian paternalism—systems trained to be helpful by default while still allowing users to override guardrails if they choose.
Cross-Book Connection: Influence explores how defaults and suggestions shape behavior without eliminating choice.
"Libertarian paternalism is a movement that preserves freedom while gently encouraging people to choose wisely."
3
Power of Defaults: Opt-In vs Opt-Out

The default option is incredibly powerful. Most people accept it as the recommendation and take it without question. This isn't laziness—it's a rational heuristic. The default is what smart designers recommend. Set the right default and you can achieve outcomes that would require tremendous persuasion otherwise.

Real Examples:
  • Organ donation: opt-out → 88% participation; opt-in → 4% participation (identical choice)
  • Retirement savings: default enrollment → 90% participation vs 35% with manual enrollment
  • Cookie consent: pre-checked boxes → 95% acceptance; unchecked boxes → 3% acceptance
"The default is the most powerful form of persuasion because people don't realize they're being persuaded. They think they're just accepting a recommendation, which is exactly what defaults are—recommendations disguised as neutrality."
AI Connection: LLM system prompts act as defaults—they shape model behavior without explicit user instruction. The default system prompt is the most powerful influence.
Cross-Book Connection: Atomic Habits emphasizes how environmental defaults (like having healthy food visible) change behavior.
"The default is not neutral. The default is powerful."
4
Status Quo Bias: Keep What We Have

People strongly prefer to stick with what they have, even if alternatives are better. The pain of loss looms larger than the pleasure of gain. We overweight the costs of changing relative to the benefits, creating inertia that keeps us trapped in suboptimal situations.

Real Examples:
  • Insurance policies: people rarely switch even when competitors offer better rates
  • Bank accounts: customers stay with worse banks due to switching costs (real and perceived)
  • Relationships: people stay in mediocre relationships due to comfort of status quo
"Status quo bias is why you have a gym membership you don't use, apps you don't need, and a job you're okay with instead of a job you'd love. The effort to change feels bigger than the pain of staying."
AI Connection: Model fine-tuning exhibits status quo bias—models resist changing from learned patterns even when presented with better information.
Cross-Book Connection: Thinking Fast & Slow explains loss aversion as the root of status quo bias.
"When people are faced with options, they have a tendency to stick with their current situation."
5
Mapping: Visible Choices

When choices are presented clearly—where you can see all options and their attributes laid out in a grid—people make better decisions. Mapping makes implicit options explicit and comparable. Confusion leads to inaction or poor choices; clarity enables good judgment.

Real Examples:
  • Healthcare plans: when presented as table with cost/coverage attributes, choices improve
  • College majors: students choose better when all requirements are mapped visually
  • Investment portfolios: attribute comparison table → better asset allocation decisions
"Most decisions fail not because the choices are hard, but because the choices are invisible. Show them clearly, and suddenly people can think."
AI Connection: Prompt clarity acts as mapping—explicit instructions map the choice space for language models better than vague requests.
Cross-Book Connection: Thinking Fast & Slow explores how visualization (mapping) improves judgment.
"When choices are properly mapped, people are better able to compare and select."
6
Expect Error: Design for Mistakes

People make mistakes. Rather than assuming they won't, design systems that make mistakes easy to prevent or recover from. Expect errors and build in safeguards—confirmations, undo buttons, warnings. The cost of error prevention is usually far less than the cost of the error itself.

Real Examples:
  • Gmail's "undo send" button prevents countless regretted emails
  • Software confirmations: "Are you sure you want to delete?" saves data
  • Surgical checklists: simple error-catching systems reduce mortality by 30%
"Don't design systems assuming people are careful. Design systems assuming people are human and will make mistakes. Then make the mistakes recoverable."
AI Connection: Guardrails and safety measures in AI systems are designed to expect and prevent errors, with rollback mechanisms to recover.
Cross-Book Connection: Thinking Fast & Slow explains why we're prone to errors and why systems must compensate.
"Assume people will make mistakes and design your system accordingly."
7
Feedback: Close the Loop

People adjust behavior when they can see the consequences of their actions. Without feedback, we can't learn or improve. Feedback that is immediate, clear, and understandable drives behavior change better than any lecture or incentive.

Real Examples:
  • Smart meters: showing real-time energy consumption reduces usage by 15%
  • Fitness trackers: seeing calories burned increases exercise compliance
  • Score immediately after test: students learn faster than waiting weeks
"You can't hit a target you can't see. That's why feedback is the nudge that works—it shows people their actual impact, and most people naturally adjust toward better behavior."
AI Connection: RLHF trains models by providing feedback on outputs, allowing continuous improvement. Without feedback, models can't learn what humans actually want.
Cross-Book Connection: Atomic Habits emphasizes feedback loops as essential for habit formation.
"Feedback is a powerful tool for changing behavior."
8
Structure Complex Choices: Break It Down

When choices are complex, break them into smaller, manageable pieces. Guide people through a sequence of decisions rather than overwhelming them with all options at once. Structure reduces cognitive load and improves decision quality.

Real Examples:
  • Wizards/step-by-step forms: increase completion rates vs single-page forms
  • College selection process: filter by location → academics → affordability
  • Investment setup: choose budget first, then risk tolerance, then specific funds
"Nobody wants to choose between 500 mutual funds. But choose between 'conservative' and 'aggressive'? Done. Then choose between options within that category? Easy. Structure turns overwhelm into clarity."
AI Connection: Prompt engineering breaks complex tasks into steps—chain-of-thought prompts structure AI reasoning into manageable pieces.
Cross-Book Connection: Thinking Fast & Slow explains why breaking choices into steps reduces System 2 load.
"When faced with complex decisions, structure the choice problem into manageable pieces."
9
Incentives: Understand Hidden Motives

Incentives are powerful because they align behavior with self-interest. But most incentive systems create unintended consequences because they reward the wrong behavior. Understand the actual incentives in a system, not just the stated ones.

Real Examples:
  • Doctors paid per visit → unnecessary visits; paid by outcomes → better care
  • Teachers graded by test scores → teaching to the test; graded by growth → real learning
  • Fuel efficiency rewards → more dangerous cars; safety ratings → actually safer
"You get what you incentivize. If you pay people by output, they maximize output quality be damned. If you pay them by satisfaction, suddenly they care. Incentives are a honest mirror of what you actually value."
AI Connection: Training objectives create incentives for AI systems. Reward model gaming is what happens when the incentive (loss function) doesn't align with actual goals.
Cross-Book Connection: Thinking Fast & Slow explores how incentives shape judgment and attention.
"Never forget that humans are driven by incentives, and that sometimes the stated incentives mask the real incentives."
10
Social Nudges: What Others Do

Descriptive norms (what people actually do) are incredibly influential. When people see that others engage in a behavior, they're more likely to do it too. This is more powerful than hectoring or appeals to duty. Simply showing "most guests reuse their towels" increases reuse by 26%.

Real Examples:
  • Energy conservation: "Your neighbors use less energy than you" reduces consumption
  • Towel reuse in hotels: "Most guests reuse towels" increases reuse by 26%
  • Tax compliance: "Most people pay taxes honestly" increases payment vs threats
"Tell someone they should reuse towels and they think you're cheap. Show them that everyone else does and suddenly they're an environmentalist. Social proof is more powerful than logic."
AI Connection: Models learn from training data representing humanity's "norms." The most common patterns in training data become the model's default behavior.
Cross-Book Connection: Influence emphasizes social proof; Nudge shows how to use it ethically.
"Descriptive norms are more powerful than injunctive norms."
11
Disclosure & Transparency: Show the Sausage

Transparency builds trust. When information is hidden or unclear, people become skeptical and make worse decisions. Full disclosure—including limitations, fees, and conflicts—helps people make informed choices and reduces regret afterward.

Real Examples:
  • Investment fund fees: disclosed upfront reduce investor regret and churn
  • Medical procedures: disclosed risks actually increase patient satisfaction
  • Social media: transparent algorithms increase user trust vs mysterious feeds
"The sausage looks disgusting when you see how it's made. So most companies hide it. But hiding it makes people trust you even less when they inevitably find out."
AI Connection: Explainability in AI systems increases user trust. Black-box models seem manipulative; transparent reasoning seems fair.
Cross-Book Connection: Thinking Fast & Slow explores how information framing affects trust and decision-making.
"Transparency is the best policy. It builds trust and enables better decisions."
12
Cooling Off Periods: Sleep on It

High-involvement decisions made in the heat of emotion often lead to regret. Requiring a cooling-off period before finalizing big decisions (purchases, contracts, policies) gives people time to reflect and reconsider. This simple nudge dramatically reduces buyer's remorse and bad decisions.

Real Examples:
  • Mortgage cooling-off periods: required delays reduce defaults and complaints by 10%+
  • Online shopping: "Save for later" reduces impulse buys; many abandoned carts never purchased
  • Divorce: mandatory waiting periods reduce filings by 20% (many reconcile during cooling-off)
"The cooling-off period is just asking yourself tomorrow morning 'Did I really need that?' in a legal framework. Half the time the answer is no."
AI Connection: Temperature settings in language models act as cooling-off periods—higher temperature = more randomness/creativity; lower temperature = more rational consistency.
Cross-Book Connection: Thinking Fast & Slow explains why emotions hijack decision-making and why cooling off helps.
"Offer a cooling-off period. You might prevent a decision you'd later regret."
